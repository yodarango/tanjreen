**Daniel In Babylon**

Daniel’s First Appeal: More Vegetables

The first chapter of Daniel is set during the initial phase of captivity for Daniel and his three friends. The text begins by giving the reader a timestamp of when the events occur—in the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim, king of Judah. This ties the events to the Babylonian conquest of Egypt at Carchemish that year. Though Babylon primarily wanted to eliminate Egypt’s support for the Assyrian Empire and Assyria's control over the area of Palestine and Syria, the spoils of war allowed Babylon to take booty and people from the more minor powers in the region, such as Syrians, Phoenicians, Egyptians, and Judeans.

The story unfolds dramatically, as Daniel and his friends were marched some nine hundred miles, stripped of their ethnic identity, and likely emasculated. They were given new names, enrolled in the Babylonian administration for three years, and feted at the royal court.

Then the king ordered Ashpenaz, the chief of his officials, to bring in some young men from the Israelites, without any physical defect, showing aptitude for every kind of learning, well-informed, quick to understand, and qualified to serve in the king’s palace. They were to be taught the language and literature of the Babylonians. The king assigned them a daily amount of food and wine from his table.

Daniel, whose name means “God is my judge,” was given the name Belteshazzar, meaning “Bel (Marduk) protect his life.” Hananiah, whose name means “The Lord shows grace,” was renamed Shadrach, meaning “command of Aku,” a Sumerian moon-god. Mishael, meaning “Who is what God is,” became Meshach, meaning “Who is what Aku is.” Azariah, meaning “The Lord helps,” was renamed Abednego, meaning “servant of Nego/Nebo.”

Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the royal food and wine, and he asked the chief official for permission not to defile himself this way. Now God had caused the official to show favor and sympathy to Daniel, but the official told Daniel he was afraid of the king, who had assigned the food and drink. He feared that if Daniel and his friends looked worse than the other young men, the king would have his head.

Daniel then said to the guard whom the chief official had appointed over Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, “Test your servants for ten days: Give us nothing but vegetables to eat and water to drink. Then compare our appearance with that of the young men who eat the royal food, and treat your servants in accordance with what you see.” So he agreed to this and tested them for ten days.

Daniel’s treatment at the hands of the Babylonians would have been against his will. Still, he selected a way to maintain some social boundary or separation from the other captives and the Babylonian religious and social customs by requesting a different diet. Most commentators say that Daniel decided to take a stand over the king's choice of food because it was used in pagan sacrificial rites. Many commentators seem to assume that the Babylonians used food in pagan sacrifices, and Daniel's objection to the food because of this use is a reasonable inference from New Testament passages where Paul talks about how pagan converts to Christianity were concerned about eating meat sacrificed to idols.

However, Daniel never says the food they were to be given was sacrificed to pagan gods. Some historical records indicate that the king’s choice food was first sacrificed to the gods, and leftovers were then distributed to the king and his court. More to the point, Daniel eats meat and drinks wine later in life, so he may have appealed for a temporary, albeit necessary, expedient.

Daniel may have appealed for two reasons. The first would be to respect the dietary laws of Israel by avoiding proscribed foods if Babylon served them; the second would be out of an abundance of caution about Babylonian social pressure. He likely avoided eating proscribed meat for the first reason and drinking wine for the second reason. These reasons need to be clarified to contemporary, Western, and non-Jewish readers.

First, though he would be living in a foreign land, Daniel would have wanted to adhere to Levitical laws. Such laws specified the types of food that were acceptable. Pig meat would have been forbidden for a religious Jew. Those laws also forbade a religious Israelite from eating meat with its blood or cooked with milk. We do not have records of what the Jewish religious community in exile did about the procedures for ensuring adherence to the law.

The Babylonians, however, had no such restrictions. It seems likely that Daniel was processed following Levitical laws, and that later in his career, when he was not beholden to the strictures of his training program, he may have eaten differently. Further, until more Jews were forced into Babylon, there would not have been a large enough community of like-minded people to help ensure the process remained pure.

Second, and perhaps more importantly, Babylonian practices were designed to socialize new immigrants into their religious, cultural, and political system. The process of enculturation began when the Babylonians compelled him to come to Babylon without his consent, gave him a pagan name, and likely emasculated him. Nowhere do we see Daniel resist any of these things; he would likely have been immediately killed by his overseers for such defiance. These things would have put enormous social pressure on Daniel to conform to Babylonian culture and religious practices and begin acculturating to Babylonian values. 

Giving Daniel a pagan name and feeding him the king’s food would have been done to honor him and elicit his loyalty. Notice the push of the constraints imposed on him and then the pull of the fine meats and wine he would be given—the trappings of social prestige. In addition to meat, typically unavailable to the average Babylonian, wine was imported and likely only available to the rich and the king's court members. Wine was not prohibited in dietary law; but both meat and wine would have been symbols of high status in Babylonian society.

Yet Daniel wanted to stay a Jew and to resist the pressure for social conformity. He may have recognized that he would be set apart for God’s kingdom purposes for his day. Rather than speaking brashly, Daniel appeals with humility and grace.

**THE APPEAL**

Several things stand out about Daniel's appeal. First, he crafted his appeal in such a way that his political overseer could decide Daniel’s fate. Daniel did not demand that his superior accede to his request; instead, he offered Ashpenaz a viable alternative and allowed him to determine after ten days whether that alternative was satisfactory and could continue. Second, because he provided an alternative, Daniel implicitly acknowledged that Ashpenaz could lose his life by considering Daniel's request. Third, Daniel respected Ashpenaz’s authority by requesting permission to make an alternative. Daniel never acted or spoke impetuously. We see him quietly determined to prepare for contingencies; it seems reasonable to believe Daniel prepared throughout his life for the unexpected challenges he would face in Babylon. His parents likely raised him as a religious Jew, and he would have been no stranger to the writings in the Mosaic law and the prophets, including Isaiah. Isaiah had prophesied that Assyria would be God’s agent to discipline Israel, prophecies given nearly one hundred years before. Isaiah prophesied that Cyrus of Persia would be used to deliver Israel from Babylonian captivity, an event yet in the future for Daniel. Then, there were those prophets who were active just before the Babylonian armies, Jeremiah and Habakkuk. They spoke about the discipline God would impose on Judah for failing to repent of its wickedness. Habakkuk recoiled at both Judah’s wickedness and the wicked nation, Babylon, which God would use as an agent of discipline. In a series of complaints and responses, Habakkuk cried against God's agent to discipline Judah, questioning how God would use such an evil nation against one that was more righteous. God answers that Babylon will eventually be punished, but those living righteously during that time will be rewarded. Habakkuk concludes that he will wait on God’s sovereign moves in judgment on Babylon. These prophets illustrated how Daniel and his friends may have considered serving in the Babylonian court. During his trip to Babylon, Daniel likely spent much time with his three friends in prayer, study, and fellowship. We infer this by working backward from what we see in the book of Daniel later. We certainly see in the book of Daniel that they worked well together on several things. In one instance, even though Daniel appeals, they had agreed to let Daniel speak for all of them in his request not to eat the meat or drink the wine of the king's table, as Daniel asks the guard in charge of him to “test your servants” for several days. In another instance, Daniel and his three friends pray together over Daniel’s ability to interpret the king’s dream, which, against the odds, he can do, saving his own life and the lives of his friends, as well as all of the court shamans. Once he successfully recalled and interpreted the dream, Daniel requested that his three friends receive promotions as part of the rewards Nebuchadnezzar offered him. The king granted Daniel's request, and Daniel's friends were ready for a promotion, as was Daniel. He had already secured their consent, or their relationship was such that he could freely ask the king on their behalf.

We also see that in the middle of this, Daniel resolved not to defile himself. Daniel and his friends likely prayed and fasted, petitioning God for an approach to remain “set apart” for his kingdom's purposes. Daniel's kingdom purposes were only enabled by seeking God's will. He resolved to stand ready to act as God directs, into a path or future that seems similar to the resolution of seeking God's will to avoid defilement, which Daniel had plenty of. Psalm 1:7 says, “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and instruction.” King Solomon warns that acquiring wisdom requires effort and instruction. Proverbs 2:1-6 emphasizes that one must first obtain the fear of the Lord and then follow certain conditions. It states, “My son, if you accept my words and store up my commands within you, turning your ear to wisdom and applying your heart to understanding—indeed, if you call out for insight and cry aloud for understanding, and if you look for it as for silver and search for it as for hidden treasure, then you will understand the fear of the Lord and find the knowledge of God. For the Lord gives wisdom, and from his mouth come knowledge and understanding.” James 1:5 says, “If any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to you.”

Notice, too, that the text states, “Now God had caused the official to show favor and sympathy to Daniel.” Have you ever requested that of God—for him to cause those over you to show favor and compassion to you? The text doesn’t say Daniel made that request, but he may have made it during his prayer. He certainly contributed to receiving the favor and compassion of Babylonian authorities by having already committed to God's kingdom purposes for his day. He was utterly incapable of accomplishing this alone, as are we. Daniel, in his weakness, let God work through him, and God must be involved in and through our weakness. At the very least, Daniel contributed to the Babylonian authorities’ favorable view of him by establishing a good relationship with his supervisors, obedience to their authority, and discipline in his work. Without that obedience and relationship, it is unlikely he would have gotten an audience with Ashpenaz to appeal.

Daniel never displays any animosity or revenge toward the Babylonian authorities that stripped him of his family, homeland, religion, and likely his ability to sire offspring. We discussed earlier how Daniel may have learned to navigate the challenges he faced. While God naturally graced Daniel with deliverance from such hardships, it seems more likely that the process he went through allowed him to reflect and recover before making this appeal. Third, Daniel made his appeal in concert with his three friends, which suggests great coordination on their part and, thus, the strength of their collective resolve. Daniel made no demands of his superiors; instead, he thoughtfully offered a genuine alternative to the king’s orders to the chief official. He determined that he needed to avoid defiling himself, whether because he believed that requesting a vegetable diet alone could help him and his friends be “set apart” from the other captives entering the king’s service, or perhaps both at the same time. Was the king’s order for those entering his service intended to shame and pollute them? This seems unlikely, as he would be polluting everyone in his service. Instead, the Babylonian court offered the choicest food and drink, not generally available to the average Babylonian, to ensure the health and success of those entering the king’s service and to confer some “status” on his servants. The king wanted people to succeed in gaining help to administer the empire. Daniel's alternative would accomplish the objectives of the king’s order to make the servants healthy. In fact, at the end of the trial period of ten days, they were said to “look healthier and better nourished” than the others. The appeal Daniel makes also allows his alternative to be tested publicly. Daniel believed God would sovereignly help him in this test, and his dependence on God was demonstrable. He now puts his trust in the sovereign God to the test, not in rebellion to political control but in reliance on God alone. The test was not subjective either. He didn’t ask a biased opinion; he encouraged the official to see whether the alternative Daniel proposed worked and evaluate the results by comparing his three friends against the others in the king's service.

The fear of the chief official was evident. Daniel likely respected the commander and understood that the king expected complete obedience from his servants. Failure to carry out the king’s orders would likely result in punishment. By easing the official’s fear of failing to carry out the king's commands, Daniel accomplished his own purpose and that of the king's.

From these observations about his request, we can infer that Daniel made a righteous appeal in an appealing and righteous manner. Yet, Daniel didn’t shame the shamans, even though they worshipped wooden idols that could not speak and practiced a profession that offered no lasting value. While the Babylonians were famous for developing astronomy, it remained subordinate to their astrology. The whole cadre of magicians, astrologers, enchanters, and sorcerers was a caste of professional religious people who did everything from reading the entrails of dead animals to anticipating success or failure on the battlefield and interpreting the omens of human lives based on the positions of the stars. They also included magic. Historian Will Durant explains some of these practices.

Daniel consistently displayed respectful obedience to political authority and never criticized the unrighteous practices of the Babylonians, even though such practices were outlawed for Israel in the Mosaic law. Magic formulas for the elimination of demons, the avoidance of evil, and the prevision of the future constitute the largest category in the Babylonian writings found in the library of Ashurbanipal. Some of the tablets are manuals of astrology; others are lists of seers.

In Babylonian society, religion, administration, art, and warfare were intertwined and subordinated to their religious worldview to a degree we cannot imagine today. Leaders relied on these advisors for all significant decisions. This was true for the Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, and later the Romans, who relied on their readings of events, particularly war decisions. The decision to go to war was particularly fraught with uncertainty, leading to considerable use of divination, looking to the gods for information that human resources could not provide. As a result, individuals who could correctly advise on these actions were highly valued and could expect rich rewards from the kings they served.

Perhaps Daniel pitied the Babylonians for their religious beliefs. Maybe he, like Paul at the Areopagus, understood that pagan religious beliefs, though of human origin, might reflect a profound yearning for something more powerful than what life offers. He understood what Paul recognized about the pagans of his era: they were certainly closer to the truth than they realized.

The account of the great empire reflects the expression of the religion of which they were a part. It might suggest a people serene in the cultivation of their fertile land and the cult of their gods. But we must not forget the great quantity of magic and divination they employed in an effort to foresee and influence the ceaseless and uncertain course of fate, the ills that threatened their existence. The Babylonians, like the Sumerians, believed in retribution for good and evil deeds in this life. The underworld was a place of darkness, where its inhabitants dragged out a wretched existence, drinking dirty water and eating dust.

No wonder Daniel didn’t shame them. He trusted God and knew he was fortunate to be alive and to serve in the court of Babylon. Daniel could execute God's kingdom purposes in an imperfect political kingdom. He didn't expect righteous behavior from people who didn’t know God.

Finally, Daniel diligently applied himself to his appeal. Early in the Babylonian court, they looked for several qualities in recruits for the king’s service. They had to be young men without any physical defect, handsome, showing aptitude for every kind of learning, well-informed, quick to understand, and qualified to serve in the king's palace. In addition to being perfect physical specimens and objectively handsome by the culture’s standards, the promising recruits had to pass tests to determine their intellectual aptitude for serving as advisors in the king’s court.

Today, we use various measures to enter colleges, graduate schools, medical schools, law schools, and even the military. What tests the Babylonians employed, we may never know. It seems likely that the candidates were asked a series of questions about their understanding of the world, how well they grasped instruction, and their potential to serve in various positions of administration. We know more about the language and literature of the Babylonians than about the tests they used to assess aptitude.

The Babylonians preserved their predecessors’ traditions, culture, and religion. The Chaldeans made remarkable developments in astronomy and provided significant political figures during the Babylonian Captivity. Some of the art on the gates of Babylon was later developed into cuneiform writing on dried clay tablets, initially designed for commercial purposes. This writing eventually became the official language for diplomacy, scholarship, and religion. It required several years of hard work to master.

During the mid-eighth century BC, the Assyrian Tiglath-Pileser II introduced Aramaic as the lingua franca of the empire, which also became the common language of those subordinate to the Assyrian Empire. Eastern Aramaic, the language throughout the region, differed from the Palestinian dialect of Judea, but it was relatively easy to learn. So, Daniel and his friends had to learn the dialect of Eastern Aramaic, Akkadian cuneiform, and likely Sumerian. Learning languages takes a great deal of effort. 

The modern Defense Language Institute trains military personnel in foreign languages, which may help guide us. A friend of mine studied in an intensive program for thirteen months to become fluent in Russian. While studying Russian, students also learn about Russian culture, history, customs, and practices. At the end of the thirteen months of intensive training, students are tested on a five-point scale for their proficiency, with five being “functionally native proficiency.” Typically, the military trains its students not so much to interact with others in their culture but to staff listening posts worldwide to understand what others are saying and thinking.

After thirteen months of intensive training, my friend could interpret the Russian military language but was not fluent enough to speak Russian as a native. He would likely have been rated four to five in listening and three in conversation. Daniel and his friends likely became very proficient at being scribes with Sumerian and cuneiform, and at least Daniel became fluent in Eastern Aramaic, for half of his book was written in it. In addition to the literature and languages, our young friends likely also learned about administering an empire.

Because Babylon sees little rain but sits astride the two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, the Babylonians kept detailed records of floods and irrigation. They also kept records on diplomacy and war. They recorded their architectural achievements, especially under Nebuchadnezzar, who began a vast building campaign that astounded the ancient world. Daniel and his friends would likely have been required to acquaint themselves with this rich tradition. They also had to learn Babylonian science and mathematics. As recently as 747 BC, the Chaldeans became more prominent in Babylon’s political history when Nabonassar, a Chaldean, ascended the throne. By then, the Chaldeans had established highly accurate records of the times and motions of the stars, so much so that they began to keep very accurate records for the various lines of kings. Later, the Greeks based their astronomy on the Chaldean understanding. Astronomy depended upon learning mathematics. All of their science, however, was subordinate to their religious understanding. It has been said that the Chaldeans, in particular, and Babylonians, in general, looked at the world to discover how things worked with religious undertones. In their records of the motions of the stars and the seasons, they also included detailed lists of floods, plagues, diseases, and other phenomena. They began to accumulate detailed descriptions of what animal activities meant for them—for example, what might happen when a dog peered into the temple or sat down by a king and what those actions portended for the future. 

It's almost impossible to know what Daniel thought about Babylonian religion because it was so intimately tied up with the Babylonian understanding of the world, politics, and administration. Yet we know he and his friends remained faithful to God while studying and gaining mastery over the beneficial aspects of their education. These young men worked extremely hard at their studies. They were at a ripe age, too, for modern neuroscience shows that from age twelve to twenty-four is when the adolescent brain is pruning and growing new neurons at an accelerated rate in preparation for the rest of life. The brain at that age undergoes a radical and rapid transformation. Their diligent studying paid off. At the end of their three-year graduate program in Babylonian administration, our friends passed with flying colors. According to the text, when Nebuchadnezzar or an appointed servant evaluated them, they were found to be an order of magnitude better than the magicians and the enchanters. God certainly equipped them, for in these four young men, God gave knowledge and understanding of all kinds of literature and learning.

This is where God and humans intersect when, by diligent obedience to Him, God holds those who excel in the tasks He gives them. At this young age, Daniel presented his appeal with less ego and more expertise than most of us do most of the time. Would that we practiced this conversational strategy with others in our day-to-day living. By seeking permission from Ashpenaz, Daniel asserts his needs while respecting the needs of others—in this case, the chief of eunuchs. Further, he does not say the Babylonians will defile him; he says he does not want to defile himself with the king’s choice of food.

Daniel expertly phrased his appeal in another way. He never demanded success, nor did he demand changes in others. He lets Ashpenaz determine whether the diet worked. After talking to Ashpenaz, Daniel submits the proposal to the guard appointed over him and his friends. The guard was likely given guidance to allow Daniel’s proposed diet and to evaluate the results. We do not know whether the guard or Ashpenaz kept the extra portions of the king’s rations for themselves, which indeed would have been a temptation for either or both. Daniel submitted to the legitimate political authority of Babylonian officials throughout this process. While he determined that the best way to remain set apart for God's kingdom purposes in Babylon was to appeal respectfully, he also made it clear that he would not defile himself. 

Daniel wanted to have some separation from the political society but not a separation that would shame members of that society. At the same time, it seems likely that Daniel wouldn't hold a grudge against anyone in political authority should the appeal fail. Daniel refused to live with anger and bitterness toward the Babylonians despite all they had done to him. My thesis is that Daniel led an exemplary life throughout this first appeal. Because he had, Daniel's benevolence in the dilemma should encourage us to follow his example. He navigated a situation that was imposed on him. Daniel’s dilemma is our dilemma: how to obey political authority while avoiding becoming absorbed by that political authority. God established political authority after the fall to restrain evil expression or allow for the rise of tyrannies. 

We are obliged to remain obedient and respectful. However, political authority is a concession to human sin. It is essential, but not supremely important, to the believer. God's kingdom transcends human political systems. Daniel wisely navigated the dilemma of obedience without absorption into the political order. He lived for God's kingdom first while remaining obedient to human authority. In his choice, he would remain dependent on God for his survival or be willing to die to stay faithful.

Quite a few books have been published lately that use “The Daniel Diet” as the basis for healthy living. However, after reading a few, most did not evaluate what Daniel may have eaten. They may have taken their cues from the English translation of the words in the Bible but not from any records of Babylon. That is not to say that such books are worthless; many offer sound advice on healthy eating and living. But Daniel's diet was a little different for him than what the books model. The average Babylonian rarely ate meat, and the poor could not afford it. Babylon, however, was the agricultural breadbasket of the ancient world. Scholars have determined from cuneiform records what kinds of foods the Babylonians consumed. They made unleavened bread, and barley provided the raw ingredients for beer, which only the upper-class people would have had regularly with their meals. The date palm featured prominently in Babylonian diets. The Babylonians also harvested pulses, primarily chickpeas, almonds, and pistachios from the hills. They would have had a variety of vegetables such as onions, leeks, turnips, lettuce, and cucumbers. Pomegranates were the most common fruits, along with dates and plums.

They would have had a variety of foods such as apples, figs, and pears, along with dishes that included cress, mustard, cumin, and seeds.

There are several key things to note about this diet. It would have consisted of unprocessed foods, which are often referred to as "superfoods." These foods would have been high in protein and contain appropriate fats. It would have been very high in soluble and insoluble fibers. More importantly, this diet would have been rich in nutrition, including vitamins and minerals, promoting good digestive health, which is vital for a young person entering the years in which their brains would be undergoing profound changes. A third thing to note is that there would be profound health advantages from such a diet. Allium vegetables, including leeks, garlic, and onion, have antibacterial and antiviral properties. Barley is packed with antioxidants, fiber, and vitamins. Almonds are also suitable for brain health and protein. Many of these foods would have had high concentrations of antioxidants. Combined, this diet would have been conducive to longer life expectancy with far lower risks of coronary heart disease, cardiovascular disease, certain kinds of cancer, and other chronic diseases.

Based on what we know from the Babylonian scribal records and paleobotanical specimens, Daniel's diet would have been extremely good. His diet would have been very similar to the list of seven species outlined first in Deuteronomy for the new land overflowing with abundant resources, consisting of wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and date honey. All of these, except olives, were produced in Babylon and were likely familiar to a religious Jew. Olives and olive oil would have been imported. No wonder Daniel served as long as he did in Babylon and the first year of the Persian Empire. He would likely have been in his eighties by then. Daniel’s success with his first appeal will lead to further success with his second.

Upon closer inspection, it appears that the chapters overlap. We know from history that Daniel and his friends entered service during the period from 605 to 601 BC, when Daniel continued to serve Babylon until the first year of King Cyrus, which followed the Persian conquest of Babylon. Daniel would serve in various administrative capacities in two empires for nearly sixty-two years.

What transpires reveals there are good reasons why, at the end of the first chapter, Nebuchadnezzar finds Daniel and his friends far better in knowledge and wisdom than the magicians and enchanters of his whole realm at their graduation in 602. In his second regnal year, the king directly observed this young foreign student’s wisdom and godly character. He saw Daniel successfully interpret the king’s troubling dream and compared this success to the manifest failure of all the other wise men in the face of the king’s wrath and commands. Only Daniel had intended to fulfill the king’s wishes, whereas the others sought to find a way out of the dilemma posed by the king.

The context for Daniel’s second appeal is found in the second chapter. During the second year of his reign, Nebuchadnezzar had a series of dreams, likely nightmares, that caused him to lose sleep. We don't know how many dreams he had or for how long they persisted, but they troubled the king. Dreams and visions were significant in the religion of the Babylonians and could portend major successes or failures. He needed to understand the meaning of his dreams. Because this dream deeply troubled him, he sent for Babylon’s professional “wise” men to help him understand it.

The wise men, magicians, enchanters, sorcerers, and astrologers (or Chaldeans) fully subordinated their education and learning to their religious beliefs. These “wise” men may have wanted to coach Nebuchadnezzar regarding how administration was done in Babylon.

The text describes Nebuchadnezzar’s summons to the wise men of Babylon. From the second chapter, the book is written in Aramaic, as these passages reflect the language of the time. The astrologers answer the king with, “May the king live forever!” and then query the king about the dream. However, Nebuchadnezzar does not reveal the dream to the wise men; instead, he insists that they tell him both the content of the dream and its interpretation. Did Nebuchadnezzar doubt their competence to advise him? The text does not let on. However, after their initial response, he says that he has decided that they must first tell him the content of the dream before he will believe the interpretation or he will have them killed and their homes destroyed. 

They answer again a second time that they need the king to tell them the dream's content before they would interpret it. Nebuchadnezzar becomes convinced that these wise men are stalling for time, perhaps conspiring against him. He tells them there can be only one result should they fail to comply with his request. While the “wise men” group is not a bureaucracy in the modern sense, they tend to behave like one. Though they are composed of disparate people with different skills, they unite to constrain the king's request to something manageable for them.

In their third reply, they inadvertently and unknowingly set the stage for God to intervene dramatically on behalf of Daniel in the unfolding drama. The astrologers answer the king, “There is no one on earth who can do what the king asks!” They emphasize their point: “No king, however great and mighty, has ever asked such a thing of any magician, enchanter, or astrologer.” They add, “What the king asks is too difficult.” Their response infuriates King Nebuchadnezzar, and he flies into a rage by ordering the execution of all the wise men of Babylon.

The text reveals that Daniel and his friends were still in school and not yet part of the Babylonian wise men, but they, too, were being sought. When Arioch, the commander of the king's guard, went out to put to death the wise men of Babylon, he explained to Daniel what had happened. At this, Daniel went in to the king to request time so that he might interpret the dream for him. Then Daniel returned to his house and explained the matter to his friends Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. He urged them to plead for mercy from the God of heaven concerning the mystery so that he and his friends might not be executed with the rest of the wise men of Babylon. During the night, the mystery was revealed to Daniel in a vision. Then Daniel praised the God of heaven and said:

“Praise be to the name of God forever and ever; wisdom and power are his. He changes times and seasons; he deposes kings and raises up others. He gives wisdom to the wise and knowledge to the discerning. He reveals deep and hidden things; he knows what lies in darkness, and light dwells with him.”

I thank and praise you, God of my ancestors. You have given me wisdom and power. You have made known to me what we asked of you. You have made known to us the dream of the king.

Then Daniel went to Arioch, whom the king had appointed to execute the wise men of Babylon, and said to him, "Do not execute the wise men of Babylon. Take me to the king, and I will interpret his dream for him."

The king asked Daniel, also called Belteshazzar, "Are you able to tell me what I saw in my dream and interpret it?" As you were lying there, O king, your mind turned to things to come, and the revealer of mysteries showed you what is going to happen. 

Now we will interpret it to the king. 

Some historical background will help us here. Nabopolassar, a Chaldean, seized the throne of Babylon in 625 BC and struggled until 612. It took nearly a decade for Nabopolassar to gain control in Babylon. The Assyrians had ruled Babylon for a very long time and were unwilling to give it up without help. The Medes represented a relatively new power in the Mesopotamian region. In 614, Nabopolassar signed a treaty with the Median king, Cyaxares, and arranged a marriage between his son Nebuchadnezzar and the Median king’s granddaughter, Amyitis. 

In 612, a coalition of Babylonians, Medes, and Scythians laid siege to Nineveh, the last stronghold of the Assyrian Empire. After three months, the city fell. The remnant of the Assyrians moved to southern Turkey until the Babylonians established a garrison there in 610 BC. The Babylonians defeated the small army at Carchemish in the spring and summer of 605 and defeated the remnants on both sides.

The historian Matt Waters argues that we are to move from Assyrian descriptions of the Medes as dependent city-lords to the Medes as a unified force that was able to unleash significant military action against Assyria.

Throughout their early history, the Medes sought to secure essential trade routes and were known for their capable horsemen. After defeating Assyria, the Medes left Babylon alone while they ventured north into modern Turkey to conquer Urartu and Lydia. However, Nebuchadnezzar may have had some forebodings about Median ambition late in his reign, as he built a great defensive wall, the Median Wall, to keep out barbarian tribes and to make attacks from the north and east more difficult. Certainly, the Medes would play an important role in the eventual destruction of Babylon in 539. Could his dream have portended Median ascendancy over Babylon?

The Medes figured prominently in earlier prophecies. We see Isaiah, who wrote about one hundred years before our story, depict them, and Jeremiah, a near contemporary of Daniel, also spoke of them. In all cases, the prophecies pertain to the eventual destruction of Babylon. While Babylon was not a great power during the early prophecies, it was an essential outpost of the Assyrian Empire. Some critics of the Bible chide the prophets for incorrectly identifying the Medes as the power that will eventually defeat Babylon. But to the Jews at the time, the Medes were the power to behold. The Persians were a minor Iranian people and not a power until the rise of Cyrus the Great around 550 BC. But the two became so close that it’s impossible to distinguish them by then. Cyrus himself was likely half Mede and half Persian. The Medes played an important role in the rise of the Persian Empire and the eventual defeat of Babylon.

Ashpenaz, one of the court officials, also features in this story. Daniel appeals to Ashpenaz not to appoint an anonymous guard over him. We never learn the guard's name, but he must have communicated with Ashpenaz in some fashion, for after making his appeal, Daniel asks this anonymous guard to test the quality of the proposed alternative diet. 

In Daniel 2, we meet a new official positioned hierarchically somewhere between Ashpenaz and this anonymous guard. He is Arioch, the commander of the guards. Not much is told about him in the text, but it seems likely that Daniel may have developed a good reputation with both the guard and Ashpenaz. If so, that reputation would have spread to Arioch, who plays a vital role in Daniel’s second appeal. 

During the crisis of the king's anger and the subsequent efforts to manage it, Arioch gives considerable latitude to Daniel to appeal for more time and clemency. 

Daniel demonstrates wisdom and patience beyond his years. Because of his first appeal, he likely developed an excellent rapport with the people he worked with. He speaks with humility, intending to solve the king’s problem. Wisdom and respect seek solutions for problems that have no clear-cut answers.

Daniel and his friends continued the excellent rapport they had begun earlier. Because they subordinated themselves to the political authority of Babylon, they gained great influence in the king’s service. 

Daniel wisely asks why the decree was so harsh or urgent. Arioch explains the situation to Daniel, allowing him to understand the gravity of the king's order. 

Daniel does something else to prepare for his second appeal: he seeks out all the facts of the situation. Instead of listening to second or third-hand information about the king’s decision to execute all the wise men, Daniel sought out Arioch, the person tasked with implementing the order. 

Daniel and his friends are a praying community of believers. After explaining the situation to his three friends, he requests them specifically for an answer to the king’s problem, urging them to plead for God's mercy so that they will not be executed along with the other wise men of Babylon. 

To advance politically and bureaucratically, lower members of a political apparatus will frequently align themselves with the careers of senior people for protection and opportunities. Mentors are expected to help mentees advance in their careers with the reciprocal expectation that junior partners will support the aspirations of their mentors. Although they are in the king’s service, Daniel and his friends do not seek out a patron to protect them.

Perhaps because they were out of the protection of a patron within the kingdom, Daniel nonetheless made friends. However, nothing in the text indicates that he relied on human authority for advancement and protection.

Notice that in his appeal, Daniel never challenges the harshness of the king’s order. Daniel lived with the knowledge that political authority given to kings and governments includes the ability to use lethal force, whether against internal or external enemies. This does not mean every decision to use force is thereby right in God's eyes. Daniel never debates whether Nebuchadnezzar's decision was just or not. Was it because he believed the king was just? Did he realize he could not challenge the king's authority? Or was he behaving wisely under the sovereign constraints of his circumstances? We see Daniel throughout the book acting under extreme conditions.

As part of his appeal, Daniel then goes to see the king and asks for a little more time so that he might interpret the dream for him. Did he actually speak with the king? Does Arioch permit him to see the king? By all accounts, Daniel doesn’t enlist the help of Ashpenaz, the chief of the court officials, who helped Daniel with his first appeal. But Arioch must have known Daniel’s reputation through Ashpenaz and perhaps his guard. So, either Daniel went in on his own to see the king, or Arioch must have somehow had confidence in Daniel to allow him to seek a little more time from the king, perhaps hoping that Daniel could do what the professional wise men of Babylon were incapable of doing. Daniel was likely granted little time, maybe only twenty-four hours, to solve the king’s problem.

As in the interpretation of the dream, the king, or Arioch on behalf of the king, would likely have seen Daniel's new appeal as credible because of his reputation. Daniel makes several appeals. In addition to appealing to the king, Daniel appeals to his friends to pray for mercy from the God of heaven and asks them to join him in doing so. His friends could have said no. Alongside them, he appeals to God to answer his request. Daniel speaks to Arioch not to execute the wise men on behalf of the king, which Arioch could have declined. Daniel finally seeks to present the results of God's answer to the king's concerns by requesting to have an audience to share his understanding of the dream. Given how poorly the professional wise men behaved, Nebuchadnezzar could have also declined. At the very least, the king was deeply unsettled. We don’t know if there was something disarming about Daniel's approach.

What makes an appeal so appealing? An appeal signifies a request for help. But demands are less likely to elicit cooperation. It is weaker to ask or protest, as power based on our weakness is not true greatness. Daniel understands that the credit is due to God. When he speaks of the kingdom of men in the Bible, while Daniel acknowledges God’s sovereign rule, at least from reading Scripture, his revelation must have increased his understanding with greater clarity than ever before. He not only understood God's sovereign constraints on his life but now fully grasped God’s sovereign constraints on political leaders in history and into the future. How much confidence would that have given him to pursue righteous political living?

He also uses the occasion to praise God before the king without pushiness or self-righteousness. In perfect timing, the king asks Daniel, “Are you able to tell me what I saw in my dream and interpret it?” The wise men had only recently said, “There is no one on earth who can do what the king asks!” They further added that “no one can reveal it to the king except the gods, and they do not live among men.” Daniel agrees with the sentiment of the wise men by saying, “No wise man, enchanter, magician, or diviner can explain to the king the mystery he has asked about, but there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries.” He takes the focus off of himself, off the wise men, off the false gods of Babylon, and puts the focus and praise squarely on the God of heaven and history. Daniel makes it clear that he correctly answered the king because they did not have the insight that the living God provides.

After hearing from Arioch about the king's decree, Daniel requests that they pray to escape the execution of the wise men. The text indicates nothing about Daniel critiquing or criticizing the religion of the wise men or the priestly caste’s ranks and behavior. He was undoubtedly aware of the corruption that was likely endemic in Babylon. However, he likely had some inkling of God's sovereignty with Arioch’s hand from executing the wise men. Was he giving the wise men an opportunity to come to understand who the God of heaven truly is?

Daniel continues to respect and never challenge the political authority of the king. He appropriately uses the chain of command by going to the correct person, Arioch. He doesn’t try to jump over the commander charged with carrying out the king's command by going to Ashpenaz or someone else. Further, in this appeal, he only asks Arioch why the king’s decree was so harsh. The Aramaic word for harsh here can mean either “harsh” or “urgent,” a term that other translations use instead of harsh. Urgency certainly seems more appropriate to the context of the text, as the king commissioned Arioch to collect all the wise men of Babylon for execution. It’s not hard to imagine Arioch having to catch his breath to hear Daniel’s question. Arioch or the king likely only gave Daniel a very short time to petition God to solve this mystery.

Daniel also avoids using any influence he may have had. Recall that Daniel appealed to Ashpenaz previously. He didn’t try to use his relationship with Ashpenaz to avoid the fate of the wise men. Nor does it appear he used any influence or patrons to help him with the problem he faced. No, the only one Daniel relied on was the God of heaven. Throughout his second appeal, he continually sought permission by appealing to others to find out the reasons for the king’s decree, to get time from the king to pray for a solution to the mystery, to get his friends to join him in prayer to God, to seek God's wisdom on what the king dreamed and how to interpret it, and to approach the king with the resolution of the mystery.

After God reveals the mystery to Daniel, wonderful things happen. Daniel approaches Arioch and asks that he stay the execution of the wise men and allow him to present the king with God's answer. Arioch then takes Daniel to the king but tells him that he found someone among the Jewish exiles who can explain the dream. Daniel never contradicts Arioch and instead allows him to take the credit. It may sound small, but this is another hallmark of Daniel’s humility. He does not boast or take credit because the solution to the king’s nightmares is God's doing, not his own. Further, he never lords it over the wise men that he solved the king’s dreams when they couldn't. There’s no trace anywhere of self-righteousness or self-aggrandizement from Daniel.

As a young person, Daniel remained calm in the face of near-certain death by the king’s decree. Young people are neurologically more likely to react strongly in challenging, demanding, or extreme situations. Neuroscientists point out that the brain’s limbic system is more reactive to threats than the executive function of the brain. When we feel threatened, our survival mechanism reacts faster, but this can lead to impulsive decisions. Young people undergo rapid changes in their brains, and they tend to feel issues of right and wrong far more strongly than earlier or later in life. Because their limbic system fires intensely, as anyone with teenagers knows, their executive function can go offline for some time, resulting in overpowering emotions that can cloud long-term reasoning.

Yet Daniel not only displays calmness in the face of threats to his life and that of his friends but also engages the executive functioning part of his brain. He expertly phrases his appeals to ensure the maximum possibility of success. Notice how, in verse fifteen, Daniel seeks out the facts of the matter while maintaining respect for authority and never questions the right of political authority to carry out the king’s decree. He seeks out information before he makes his decision to appeal for time. He relies on facts, not limbic-system-generated interpretations of the situation, and he doesn’t rely on second and third-hand information. Daniel seeks a little time to make the interpretation known when he appeals. This would have been tricky for him, given that Nebuchadnezzar believed the wise men were playing for time. But he makes it clear that the time he needs is not open-ended, nor is he engaging in subterfuge; he seeks to make the interpretation known. He strives to fulfill both the king's request and his own desire for clarity. How refreshing would his approach be to the king, who had only recently expressed his frustration with the wise men? The king now has someone who seeks to help.

Daniel never stepped outside the bounds of his execution of political authority. Even when he tells Arioch to find someone who could interpret the dream, he later lets Arioch take credit for finding someone. He never challenges political authority or rails against the king's urgent and harsh decree. He merely responds respectfully, appealing to God through prayer for an answer to the king's troubling dreams. Nebuchadnezzar's mysterious dreams are a significant concern.

Daniel gives credit where credit is due: to the God of heaven. He praises God, who solved the dilemma for Daniel and empowered his request for the interpretation of the dream. Daniel also credits Nebuchadnezzar, telling the king that God has shown him what will happen in days to come. According to Daniel, God honored Nebuchadnezzar with the dream, and he should be grateful to the God of Heaven for having it. He praises the king for being a worthy vessel of such vital information. Nebuchadnezzar honors Daniel at the end of the chapter. He orders that an offering and incense be given to Daniel and acknowledges that Daniel serves a mighty God. He says, “Surely your God is the God of gods and the Lord of kings and a revealer of mysteries, for you were able to reveal this mystery.” Then, at some point later, he promotes Daniel to administer the province of Babylon and puts him in charge of all the wise men. We are not given a time-stamp at the end of this chapter that indicates when this occurs, except that it is after the initial offering and incense. Since Daniel was still in his second year of schooling, and we know from the end of the previous chapter that the king evaluates Daniel and his Jewish colleagues as outstanding graduates, he must have been put into such a leadership position upon his graduation, sometime around 601 BC. Still, he would have been very young, around nineteen years old. Daniel also became more vulnerable to the bureaucratic machinations of the other wise men of Babylon.

Critics of the book of Daniel usually do not care for chapters that are apocalyptic and reveal what is to come. They tend to dismiss these chapters as written later after the events, so they do not solve all the issues. This is partly because Nebuchadnezzar’s dream of the statue is apocalyptic. It reveals different kingdoms, from Babylon and on into history to include something like our day. There will be a final kingdom, one not made by human hands, that smashes the last kingdom, the kingdom of iron and clay. This is understood to be the establishment of God’s kingdom on earth, which started when Jesus announced the kingdom was at hand and would be finalized at a later time. Daniel reveals that the image in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream was a giant statue of a man. On top was a head of gold. Below the head was a chest and arms of silver. The belly and thighs of the statue were of bronze. Its legs were of iron, and its feet were partly iron and clay. These metals were well known to the ancients of this time, as they are to us today, but the clay may have been something like the clay the Babylonians fired in furnaces to make bricks for their impressive building campaign. The furnace that Daniel’s three friends will be forced to endure may have been used to fire clay bricks. 

Daniel interprets the dream. The head of gold represents Nebuchadnezzar's political reign. Daniel mentions what makes the head of gold—that is, Nebuchadnezzar’s reign—superior to the subsequent kingdoms. In verses thirty-seven to thirty-eight, Daniel says, “Your majesty, you are the king of kings. The God of heaven has given you dominion and power and might and glory; in your hands, he has placed all mankind and the beasts of the field and the birds of the sky. Wherever they live, he has made you ruler over them all. You are that head of gold.” The silver of the chest and arms represents a new political order that will be “inferior to yours.” The silver represents the Medo-Persian Empire that began under the reign of Cyrus the Great. A third kingdom is understood to be the Macedonian (sometimes called Greek) Empire and is represented by bronze and “will rule over the whole earth.” The fourth kingdom, the Roman Empire, is represented by iron and its military force. The feet of clay mixed with iron are understood to be the divided kingdoms that arise from the Roman Empire, likely those that will rule until the time God's kingdom establishes itself.

Daniel's interpretation may be puzzling in some ways. In what sense was Babylon superior to subsequent gentile empires? As we will see below, the land area under the sovereign control of the various empires depicted in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream starts to expand slowly (with Babylon), expands greatly under Cyrus, shrinks somewhat under Alexander the Great, and never achieves the same extent under Rome. The size of these empires is significant. Babylon is by far the smallest of the four empires listed. As the first in history to jump above more than two million square miles, the Persian Empire is the largest. Cyrus may have supersized his empire by innovating satrapies (provinces) and delegating limited power to local provinces. Alexander the Great only conquered about ninety percent of the land area of the Persian Empire. Separately, Rome carved out its own smaller empire, and in the east, it did so at the expense of the leftover Greek empires. On a map, it is apparent that these four empires’ geographical expansion was horizontal, which is typical for empires throughout history. Something like eighty percent of all historical empires before the age of steamships and newer technology expanded in this way, as they found it much easier to project military and political power within the same ecological zone.

The peak size of the empires suggests something interesting as well. Babylon's peak year of size was 562 BC, at the death of Nebuchadnezzar. Several more kings ruled until Persia conquered Babylon in 539 BC, but none had the vigor of the second king. The peak size for Persia was in 500 BC, about thirty years after the death of Cyrus, during the reign of his greatest successor, Darius. The peak size of the Macedonian Empire was in 323 BC, at the death of Alexander.

His heirs divided the kingdom into four areas, two of which subsequently emerged in the Macedonian world in the West. Rome was at its peak in AD 117, nearly fifty years after the founding of the Roman Empire in Jerusalem and many years after the events described in Daniel 2:36. This, along with something Daniel suggests, indicates that the coherence and greatness of these human kingdoms had to do with the effectiveness of political rule during these times.

Nebuchadnezzar ruled over his kingdom with almost absolute efficiency, while other rulers governed their empires less effectively. Cyrus and others had varying degrees of authority. Nebuchadnezzar was able to exercise complete sovereignty, as indicated in Daniel 2:12 and 48, and he changed the laws of the Medes and Persians. Alexander the Great's real genius lay in military conquest, but he did not live very long after he conquered his empire.

While still an empire, Rome had to contend with increasing checks on the emperors’ power. Over time, the relative power of all kingdoms depicted in this dream diminishes until other human kingdoms conquer them. Note that the statue in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream also ignores other powerful states and empires throughout history. During the peak years of Roman rule, when Rome had a land area of some 1.93 million square miles, Han China had over 2.5 million square miles. Subsequently, other empires have come to occupy much greater land areas, including China, Russia, Canada, Brazil, and Australia. 

The central focus of the statue, however, is on the nature of human kingdoms. It is evident that people throughout history can expect trouble from these gentile kingdoms, which, while regarded by some as powerful, pursue the rulers’ interests, not God's. They never pursue God’s interests except incidentally, when God directs their hearts, as we have seen before. And, as Revelation 13 and 17 make clear, these rulers fight against the Lord himself and his followers.

Daniel interprets the dream to mean the duality of human kingdoms, which possess both strength (iron) and weakness (clay). They will have a hard time maintaining their unity, as the toes of the statue were partly iron and partly clay, indicating that the people will be a mixture and will not hold together. The author suggests that this time will be far more brittle than during the reign of Nebuchadnezzar and even of the subsequent kingdoms in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream. 

Daniel’s interpretation of the apocalyptic dream seems to suggest that it will become more complex and challenging for gentile regimes to hold on to their legitimacy and authority, even while appearing ruthlessly strong toward their citizens. We see something similar from time to time today. The Soviet Union, which looked powerful in the 1970s and 1980s, crumbled quickly once the political leadership lost its legitimacy. Like dominoes, a whole series of subordinate nations followed when Soviet legitimacy was called into question. China exhibits elements of both harsh power and the fragility of legitimacy, as seen during the Tiananmen crisis.

In chapter 2, we read about Daniel's political wisdom. He remained politically subordinate yet supportive of the authorities, and, most importantly, he made appeals to God. He found a way to flourish in an otherwise hostile political system by remaining committed to God's kingdom purposes.

The year 539 BC brought tumult for Daniel. He recorded significant events that year that would transform him, his fellow Jews, international politics, and the kingdom of God he served. That year finds Daniel serving in high political office, first in the Babylonian Empire's final days and then in the Persian Empire's early days, which are events foreshadowed in Daniel 2, 7, and 8. In Daniel 5, we saw that he was called out by the ruling faction of Belshazzar’s political officers to interpret some strange handwriting on the wall. The evening of the party where Daniel announced the reading of the handwriting foretold the imminent fall of Babylon and Persia's conquest of it. Babylon did fall, changing the regional political structures of Mesopotamia and the Middle East, and Persia emerged as a much larger empire.

The new king, Darius, appointed Daniel to one of the three top administrative posts in the new Babylonian situation, now under the control of the Persian Empire. Over several months, Daniel’s political opponents conspired to discredit him in the eyes of the new king. Their trap elicits Daniel’s faithful conduct, which ultimately leads to his being thrown into a den of lions. 

In the first year of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign, Jeremiah had prophesied that Judah would be made captives for the persistent rebellion of the whole society. By the way Daniel writes in his book, he seems to have discovered this prophecy for the first time. Jeremiah’s prophecies were sent to Babylon, where Daniel could have taken them. Jeremiah 29, for example, is an open letter to the Jewish exiles who live in Babylon, encouraging them to prosper by serving Babylon. 

Daniel understands from reading the book of Jeremiah that the terminus for the seventy years of exile is drawing close. From Ezra 1:1-4, we learn that Cyrus gave the edict to restore Babylon's captives to their original homeland in 538 BC. Historically, some Jewish exiles began their return that year, but the full return didn't happen for many years. The Ezra passage hints at the reluctance of some to return. 

To determine the end of the seventy years of exile, we must determine when the exile began. Was it 605 BC with the deportation of Daniel and his three friends? If so, then only sixty-seven years would have passed later, with the largest deportation of 586, when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed the temple. Several hypotheses have been suggested regarding the different dates for Jeremiah’s prophecy. The first is that the seventy years began in 605, while the largest deportation didn’t occur until 586 with the destruction of the temple. 

Daniel’s understanding of these prophecies and their implications for his people is crucial as he navigates the political landscape of his time.

The destruction of the first temple occurred in 586 BC, and the second temple was completed in 516 BC. This timeline is crucial for understanding Daniel's context. The first hypothesis regarding the exile begins with Josiah, suggesting that the exile lasts exactly seventy years until Cyrus issues the edict. However, no reason is provided for this specific timeframe.

Another explanation may be found in Habakkuk 1. Habakkuk’s prophecy was likely written between 609 and 605 BC, after the death of the last righteous king, Josiah. This period coincides with when Nebuchadnezzar began conquering Judah. In this passage, the prophet laments the shameless robbery and oppression of the common people in Judah by the nobles. In Habakkuk 1:2-4, the prophet asks, “How long, O Lord, must I call for help, but you do not listen? Or cry out to you, ‘Violence!’ but you do not save? Why do you make me look at injustice? Why do you tolerate wrongdoing? Destruction and violence are before me; there is strife, and conflict abounds. Therefore the law is paralyzed, and justice never prevails. Assessing the reliability of the book of Daniel involves analyzing competing hypotheses. Scholars today hold to one of two main hypotheses about the book. Either a single author in the late sixth century BC wrote the book, or it was written during the second century BC by an anonymous Jew; the purpose of the book, according to these latter scholars, was to encourage the Jews during the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who persecuted the Jews, especially for their faith. Judaism has traditionally confirmed the former thesis since before the time of Christ, and orthodox Christianity has done so since its inception. The latter took hold after the Enlightenment in the West. Many recent scholars hold to the second-century hypothesis for various reasons. Most, though not all, argue that the book, or significant parts of it, were written during the second century to encourage the Jews when their faith was under attack by that diabolical king.

For this reason, this approach is called the late-date—or Maccabean—hypothesis. It has been argued that the book was penned during the Maccabean revolt against the Seleucid Empire in 167-64 BC. Most scholars who support this view argue that the apocalypses (revelations) were vaticinium ex eventu (after the fact), which means that Daniel's prophetic accuracy up to the time of Antiochus IV could only be explained if the anonymous author wrote it during that particular king’s reign. In other words, the book of Daniel was not prophetic, and thus, his prophecies of the future are also considered false.

Scholars from both sides of the debate present evidence to support their positions. Internal evidence comes from the book itself or from other parts of the Bible. External evidence comes from history, archaeology, or written sources. We will test the arguments and evaluate which hypotheses may be more reliable or more coherent. We will also evaluate some unstated assumptions both sides hold. Assumptions frequently direct our inquiry and may be unclear. Such assumptions can allow us to connect the dots or distort disconfirming evidence. 

For all of us, it is vital to assess the various assumptions before evaluating the hypotheses. This also includes determining which hypothesis has the least disconfirming evidence. 

The hypothesis that the author of the book was a single person situated in the late sixth century BC has the least disconfirming evidence and the most confirming evidence for it. Given this, I assess that it is “almost certainly” true that a single author, probably Daniel, wrote the book sometime in the late sixth century BC. The hypothesis that an anonymous Jew wrote the book of Daniel during the second century BC has the most disconfirming and least confirming arguments and evidence in its favor. Consequently, I assess that there is “almost no chance” that it is accurate or the best explanation of the book’s authorship. 

Arguing for the best explanation leads a dispassionate reader to accept the authorship of the book of Daniel. Four main sub-hypotheses support this assessment:

1. There is overwhelming evidence that Daniel was recognized, by name, as the book’s author by a contemporary in Babylon, subsequent Jewish scholars, several New Testament authors, a non-Christian Jewish Roman historian, and the early church fathers. Jesus acknowledged Daniel as the book’s author.

2. The book exhibits coherence and a point of view that makes it likely the work of a single author from the late sixth century BC. 

3. The author's use of various languages, including Hebrew, Aramaic, Persian, and Greek, points to a single author in the late sixth century BC. Knowledge of critical people, religion, and details of court life in Babylon would unlikely have been known with such detail after the fifth century BC—at least until modern archaeology dating from the nineteenth century AD was available.

4. This sub-hypothesis focuses on how early Jews and Christians—Jesus included—viewed the book's authorship. A near contemporary of Daniel, the prophet Ezekiel, refers to him twice in his book, probably published about thirty years after Daniel established his career and reputation in the court of Babylon. Human sources of information are considered more reliable when proven trustworthy over time. Ezekiel had this in spades. He was likely taken in the second wave of exiles around 597 BC, about seven to eight years after Daniel, who was taken in the first wave around 605 BC, and would have been intimately familiar with Daniel’s growing reputation.

In Ezekiel 14:14-20, Daniel is praised for his righteousness, and in Ezekiel 28:3, he is praised for his wisdom and ability to solve mysteries. Critics argue that the Daniel listed with Noah and Job in Ezekiel 14:14 and 20 may be someone else. However, the Dnil of Ugaritic myth does not share the characteristics of the biblical Daniel, who was a worshiper of the true God. It is far more likely that the prophet referred to is the contemporary, exiled Jew, who, like him, had established an outstanding reputation for his wisdom and righteousness in Babylon.

Contemporary Jews in exile would know of Daniel from his actions in Babylon rather than from a myth acknowledged by any other book in the Old Testament. Critics also have to ignore Ezekiel 28:3, where the prophet questions whether anyone is wiser than Daniel, who can unveil mysteries, as Daniel did throughout his book. The Dnil of myth did no such thing. Every part of Daniel's book was preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls of Qumran, which existed around 150 BC. It is highly unlikely that the community at Qumran would have regarded the book of Daniel as authentic had it been recently written or portions of it written by an anonymous Jew living near the time of Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Indeed, Jewish scholars had a test for the reliability of prophets and prophecy, which had been laid out in Deuteronomy 18:22: “If what a prophet proclaims in the name of the Lord does not take place or come true, that is a message the Lord has not spoken. The prophet has spoken presumptuously, so do not be alarmed.” As Jewish scholars noted, Daniel's prophetic accuracy until Antiochus Epiphanes was outstanding. 

The book of Daniel and other Old Testament books are part of the Tanakh in Hebrew. The Tanakh went through a process of canonization.

**Daniel In Babylon**

Scholars believe prophecy ceased in the question of the book of Daniel’s authorship. The book is categorized under the Hagiographa rather than the Prophets. According to Josephus, the Septuagint, and the Talmud, it is indicated in Jewish circles that the Prophets and Hagiographa are significant. All thirty-nine Old Testament books are the same as the twenty-four books of the Tanakh. All were considered to be inspired by God and “canonical.” In the Talmudic tradition, the book is regarded as authoritative for religious practice, and its authority is binding upon the Jewish people. Such books are to be studied and expounded upon.

Talmudic scholars examined whether it was composed under divine inspiration—that is to say, whether God spoke the words or whether He gave the words to the prophets. According to Jewish scholarship, Hebrew Scriptures in Jewish and Christian traditions were considered “inspired” and “canonical.” Some Jewish literature may be regarded as canonical but uninspired, but not the Tanakh, our Old Testament. For example, the Mishnah (exegetical material, judgments, and rulings on the law by Talmudic scholars) is considered canonical but uninspired in Jewish tradition.

Scholars considered the book of Daniel to be inspired and canonical. By the first century AD, if not before, Talmudic scholars considered all of the books of the Tanakh as canonical. There remains some uncertainty about when the canonization may have happened, as it may have occurred in stages. A fair reading of Daniel 9:2 suggests that the books were already considered canonical when Jesus, the apostles, and the early church fathers affirmed the book of Daniel as inspired and canonical. 

The New Testament understanding of inspiration is similar to the Jewish understanding. This makes sense as the early converts were Jewish. In 2 Timothy 3:16-17, the apostle Paul declares, “All Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting, and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work.” “God-breathed” is a literal translation of the Greek word theopneustos, from which we get our term “inspiration.” God actively breathed His ideas to and through the writers of the Holy Scriptures without changing their personalities, vocabulary, or idioms. The apostle Peter makes a similar claim in 2 Peter 1:20-21: “Above all, you must understand that no prophecy of Scripture came about by the prophet’s own interpretation. For prophecy never had its origin in the human will, but prophets, though human, spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.” Human will cannot prompt God’s inspiration. 

Christians applied this maxim along with other criteria to determine the inspiration and canonicity of the Old and New Testament works, and the book of Daniel has always been regarded as among them. The Apostle John in Revelation also affirmed the authenticity of the book of Daniel.

The term canon, from the Greek word meaning “rule” or “measuring stick,” refers to the standard or rule for how we are to authenticate Scripture. The early church fathers regarded Daniel as canonical, including Melito, Origen of Alexandria, Epiphanius, and Jerome, among others. An early critic was Porphyry, a third-century AD philosopher with a personal agenda against Christianity.

The book of Daniel exhibits a coherent point of view that likely makes it the work of a single author from the sixth century. A quick reminder about the structure of the book of Daniel: the first six chapters are considered Babylonian and Persian court narratives, while the last six chapters (7-12) are apocalypses or revelations. However, that is only part of the picture, as Daniel 2 documents King Nebuchadnezzar’s revelation of future kingdoms that only Daniel can understand. 

About half of the book is written in Hebrew (chapters 1-2a; 8-12), and the other half is written in Aramaic (chapters 2b-7). The simple division of the book into Hebrew narrative and apocalyptic chapters belies the complex organization. The book displays a chiastic structure, where chapters 2-7 form an outer parallel with the apocalyptic chapter. A chiasm is a literary device in which parallel arrangements create a symmetrical structure. 

In addition to Daniel’s chiastic structure for chapters 2-7, he crafts chapter 7 as the linchpin between the book’s narrative sections and the Hebrew language apocalypses. Chapters 1-12 are primarily set during Daniel's lifetime, but chapters 7-12 provide revelations of the future. Chapters 1-6 focus on God’s sovereign rule over gentile empires, while chapters 7-12 focus on Daniel's visions of the future rule of gentile empires and the suffering believers can expect until the Messiah sets up an eternal kingdom on earth. 

The structure of the book amplifies the horrific persecution the Jews will face under gentile kingdoms, up to and including the end times. The chiastic structure highlights the parallel between chapter 4, in which a proud gentile king (Nebuchadnezzar) is humbled, and chapter 5, where yet another proud gentile king (Belshazzar) is humbled. Chapters 3 and 6 parallel the righteous responses of Daniel’s friends facing death for their faith and Daniel facing his own death for his faith. 

The appeals made in the book, including establishing a solid reputation in religious ritual and corporate prayer, wisely lead to the acknowledgment of higher authority, including an appeal to God Himself.

In summary, the five appeals Daniel makes are as follows:

1. Daniel 1:8-10: Daniel appeals to the commander of officials to choose a vegetarian diet to avoid defiling himself.
2. Daniel 2:14-28: Daniel appeals to Arioch, the captain of the king’s bodyguards, to seek God’s help in revealing Nebuchadnezzar’s dreams.
3. Daniel 6:1-24: Daniel makes an appeal without words to King Darius, who was tricked by his sub-officials into framing Daniel for violating a political order.
4. Daniel 9:1-23: Daniel appeals to God for the forgiveness of Israel’s sins and is met by the angel Gabriel, who gives him further information on the future of Israel.

The author of the book uses different languages for distinction, employing ancient Aramaic and Hebrew where appropriate. The sections written in Aramaic were intended for a wider audience, including Jews and Gentiles living in Babylon, while the Hebrew portion was focused on Jewish concerns, such as the future of Judah under Gentile kingdoms.

In Daniel 12:9, the prophet is told that parts of the text were to be closed and sealed up for a future age, which raises skepticism among critics. Daniel also uses foreign "loan" words, including three Greek words for musical instruments and fifteen Persian words for politics and administration. Critics argue that the use of Greek words indicates that Daniel's writing occurred during the second century BC, during the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, as Palestine had been Hellenized since the time of Alexander the Great.

However, Greek culture and language had already spread to the Levant and Mesopotamia by the tenth century BC, long before the book of Daniel was written. Greek words for musical instruments would have been available throughout the Near East, especially with the presence of Greek mercenaries in Mesopotamia before the rise of Babylon.

Critics also point out that Daniel's detailed knowledge of Babylonian court life and critical personnel would have been unlikely to be known after the fifth century BC. Only an author in the late sixth century BC would have had such intimate knowledge. Writing in 450 BC, the Greek historian Herodotus does not mention any record of Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar, while Daniel demonstrates a more accurate understanding of Neo-Babylonian and early Achaemenid Persian history than any known historian since that time.

Furthermore, it is doubtful that a pseudonymous author would have understood the distinct political systems between Babylon and Persia. Such an author would not have known the laws that Nebuchadnezzar could enact and that Darius was powerless to change.

Only an author of the sixth century BC could have known about King Belshazzar and his place in the royal court. Historians have established that Nabonidus was the last king of the Babylonian Empire. Critics previously pointed to this as a historical error in Daniel until cuneiform tablets referring to Belshazzar as "the son of the king" were discovered. Belshazzar was appointed as regent while Nabonidus subdued his opponents. Daniel correctly noted that Belshazzar promised him promotion to third ruler in the kingdom, as Belshazzar was second to his father.

Some critics argue that Belshazzar was not Nebuchadnezzar’s son but Nabonidus’s son, failing to recognize that ancient sources often referred to successors in office as sons. 

The historical context and details presented in the book of Daniel suggest that it was written by someone with firsthand knowledge of the events and figures of the time, reinforcing the argument for its authenticity.

We are familiar with spoken prophecy, where the prophet tells the reader what can be expected. Type is an unspoken prophecy that foreshadows what will come. Types of prophecies are used throughout the Bible. The first man, Adam, foreshadows Jesus Christ, according to the apostle Paul. Their faith was in a future king.

Antiochus Epiphanes foreshadows the end times and the Antichrist, who will eventually die in Jerusalem. The final wicked ruler, Antiochus Epiphanes, in Daniel 11:36–12:13, foreshadows world history before the return of Jesus Christ. Daniel also related many prophecies for which the late-date authorship hypothesis cannot account. Several stand out as they relate to events that have occurred in history since the second century BC but before the end times. 

The first is that in both Daniel 2 and 7, Daniel points to the emergence of the fourth empire of Rome. Though Rome had been around for some time and had established a vassal protectorate in Egypt, it only took control over the Levant in 63 BC. Second, as we saw in his fourth appeal, Daniel was given a prophecy of the coming of the Messiah, which indicated that sometime during his ministry, he would be put to death. The destruction mentioned by Daniel was still in the future and was not the work of Antiochus. Finally, Daniel predicts the destruction of the temple after the Messiah was cut off, which eventually occurred in AD 70.

Many of Daniel’s other prophecies remain for the future or toward the end times. But, as Deuteronomy requires, Daniel’s history and short-term prophecies must first be proven true. Only after that can we trust his long-term, yet-to-be-fulfilled prophecies. However, just as the prophecies of the past serve as a type, Daniel's end-times prophecies are ripe for understanding. The words in the book of Daniel will not be fully understood until the time is right, and the latent information has been preserved to help the righteous understand what unfolds before their eyes.

We are at a point where conducting a Key Assumptions Check becomes essential. Each position must be evaluated to determine if the assumptions cancel each other out. Hypothesis 1 assumes that a transcendent God created the entire universe. Therefore, prophecy is possible but not likely. Hypothesis 2 believes that prophecy is impossible or a variation on it: there is no God, and that contradicts the assumption of prophecy’s truthfulness. It is, therefore, incumbent on us to evaluate the lines of evidence for each hypothesis. Did a single author write the book in the late sixth century BC, or did an anonymous Jew write it in the second century BC? 

As we have seen, there are fewer disconfirming lines of evidence favoring the late authorship hypothesis, suggesting that the book was written by a single author in the late sixth century BC. On the other hand, there are far more disconfirming items of evidence for the second-century authorship hypothesis. This strongly suggests that the assumptions, not the evidence, drive the second-century-author hypothesis. Advocates for this view would be well-served by explicitly stating their assumptions and revising their interpretation of the book’s authorship accordingly.

The Key Assumptions Check lets us know whether the evidence or our explicit or implicit assumptions drive the analysis. In our case, the results strongly suggest that Daniel authored the book in the sixth century. Just because the book of Daniel is difficult does not make it untrue. On the contrary, there is much to commend us to believe it is reliably accurate. We can be encouraged by the lines of evidence that support the argument that Daniel authored the book during the sixth century BC and not shy away from our trust in the God of the universe and the words of the Bible. Put simply, the book of Daniel stands the test and may be considered an inspired book of the Bible, which still speaks to us today.

Daniel’s first appeal occurs during the initial phase of captivity for Daniel and his three friends. The text begins by giving a timestamp of when the events occur—in the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim, king of Judah. This ties the events to the Babylonian conquest of Egypt at Carchemish that year. Though Babylon primarily wanted to eliminate Egypt’s support for the Assyrian Empire, the spoils of war allowed Babylon to take booty and people from the minor powers in the region, such as Syrians, Phoenicians, Egyptians, and Judeans. 

The story unfolds dramatically, as Daniel and his friends were marched some nine hundred miles, stripped of their ethnic identity, and likely emasculated. They were given new names, enrolled in the Babylonian administration for three years, and feted at the royal court. The king ordered Ashpenaz, the chief of his officials, to bring in some Israelites from the royal family and nobility—young men without any physical defect, handsome, showing aptitude for every kind of learning, well-informed, quick to understand, and qualified to serve in the king’s palace. They were to be taught the language and literature of the Babylonians. The king assigned them a daily amount of food and wine from his table. 

Daniel, whose name means “God is my judge,” was given the name Belteshazzar, meaning “Bel (Marduk) protect his life.” Hananiah, meaning “The Lord shows grace,” was renamed Shadrach, meaning “command of Aku,” a Sumerian moon-god. Mishael, meaning “Who is what God is,” became Meshach, meaning “Who is what Aku is.” Azariah, meaning “The Lord helps,” was renamed Abednego, meaning “servant of Nego/Nebo.”

Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the royal food and wine, and he asked the chief official for permission not to defile himself this way. Now God had caused the official to show favor and sympathy to Daniel, but the official told Daniel, “I am afraid of my lord the king, who has assigned your food and drink. Why should he see you looking worse than the other young men your age? The king would then have my head because of you.” 

Daniel then said to the guard whom the chief official had appointed over Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, “Please test your servants for ten days: Give us nothing but vegetables to eat and water to drink. Then compare our appearance with that of the young men who eat the royal food, and treat your servants in accordance with what you see.” So he agreed to this and tested them for ten days. Daniel’s treatment at the hands of the Babylonians would have been against his will. Still, he selected a way to maintain some social boundary or separation from the Babylonian religious and social customs by requesting a different diet. Most commentators say that Daniel decided to take a stand over the king's choice of food because it was used in pagan sacrificial rites.

Many commentators seem to assume that the Babylonians used food in pagan sacrifices, and Daniel's objection to the food because of this use is a reasonable inference from New Testament passages where Paul talks about how pagan converts to Christianity were concerned about eating meat sacrificed to idols in the pagan practices of Greece. However, it is worth noting that Daniel never says the food they were to be given was sacrificed to pagan gods. Some historical records from the Seleucid Period indicate that the king’s choice food was first sacrificed to the gods. Leftovers, which were plentiful, were then distributed to the king and his court. More to the point, Daniel eats meat and drinks wine later in life, so he may have appealed for a temporary, albeit necessary, expedient. 

Daniel may have appealed for two reasons. The first would be to respect the dietary laws of Israel by avoiding proscribed foods if Babylon served them; the second would be out of an abundance of caution about Babylonian social pressure. He likely avoided eating proscribed meat for the first reason and drinking wine for the second reason. These reasons need to be clarified to contemporary, Western, and non-Jewish readers. First, though he would be living in a foreign land, Daniel would have wanted to adhere to Levitical laws. Such laws specified the types of food that were acceptable. Pig meat would have been forbidden for a religious Jew. Those laws also forbade a religious Israelite from eating meat with its blood or cooked with milk. We do not have records of what the Jewish religious community in exile did to ensure adherence to the law. Those procedures later came to be called kosher. The Babylonians, however, had no such restrictions. It seems likely that Daniel's food was processed following Levitical laws, and that later in his career, when he was not beholden to the strictures of his training program, he may have eaten differently. 

Further, until more Jews were forced into Babylon, there would not have been a large enough community of like-minded people to help ensure the process remained pure. Second, and perhaps more importantly, Babylonian practices were designed to socialize new immigrants into their religious, cultural, and political system. The process of enculturation began when the Babylonians compelled him to come to Babylon without his consent, gave him a pagan name, and likely emasculated him. Nowhere do we see Daniel resist any of these things; he would likely have been immediately killed by his overseers for such defiance. These things would have put enormous social pressure on Daniel to conform to Babylonian culture and religious practices and begin acculturating to Babylonian values. Giving Daniel a pagan name and feeding him the king’s food would have been done to honor him and elicit his loyalty. Notice the push of the constraints imposed on him and then the pull of the fine meats and wine he would be given—the trappings of social prestige. In addition to meat, typically unavailable to the average Babylonian, wine was imported and likely only available to the rich and the king's court members. Wine was not prohibited in dietary law; but both meat and wine would have been symbols of high status in Babylonian society. Yet Daniel wanted to stay a Jew and to resist the pressure for social conformity. He recognized that he would be set apart for God’s kingdom purposes for his day. Rather than speaking brashly, Daniel appealed with humility and grace.

Several things stand out about Daniel's appeal. First, he crafted his appeal so that his political overseer could decide Daniel’s fate. Daniel did not demand that his superior accede to his request; instead, he offered Ashpenaz a viable alternative and allowed him to determine after ten days whether that alternative was satisfactory and could continue. Second, because he provided an alternative, Daniel implicitly acknowledged that Ashpenaz could lose his life by considering Daniel's request. Third, Daniel respected Ashpenaz’s authority by requesting permission to make an alternative. Daniel never acted or spoke impetuously. We see him quietly determined to prepare for contingencies; it seems reasonable to believe Daniel prepared throughout his life for the unexpected challenges he would face in Babylon. His parents likely raised him as a religious Jew, and he would have been no stranger to the writings in the Mosaic law and the prophets, including Isaiah. Isaiah had prophesied that Assyria would be God’s agent to discipline Israel, prophecies given nearly one hundred years before. Isaiah prophesied that Cyrus of Persia would be used to deliver Israel from Babylonian captivity, an event yet in the future for Daniel. Then, there were those prophets who were active just before the Babylonian armies, Jeremiah and Habakkuk. They spoke about the discipline that God would impose on Judah for failing to repent of its wickedness. Habakkuk also cried out against God's agent to discipline Judah, expressing his astonishment that God would use such an evil nation against one that was more righteous. Still, God answers that Babylon will eventually be punished, and that faithful living during that time will be rewarded. Habakkuk tells the faithful to wait for God's judgment to come upon Babylon. After reviewing God's historical acts of judgment, Habakkuk finally concludes that he will wait on God’s sovereign moves in judgment on Babylon. These prophets illustrated how Daniel and his friends may have considered serving in its court. 

During his trip to Babylon, Daniel likely spent much time with his three friends in prayer, study, and fellowship. We infer this by working backward from what we see in the book of Daniel later. We certainly see in the book of Daniel that they worked well together on several things. In one instance, even though Daniel appeals, they had agreed to let Daniel speak for all of them in his request not to eat the meat or drink the wine of the king's table, as Daniel asks the guard in charge of him to test your servants for several days. In another instance, Daniel and his three friends pray together over Daniel’s ability to interpret the king’s dream, which, against the odds, he can do, saving his own life and the lives of his friends, as well as all of the court shamans. Once he successfully recalled and interpreted the dream, Daniel requested that his three friends receive promotions as part of the rewards Nebuchadnezzar offered him. The king granted Daniel's request, and Daniel's friends were ready for a promotion, as was Daniel. He had already secured their consent, or their relationship was so well established that he could freely ask the king on their behalf. We also see that in the middle of this, Daniel resolved not to defile himself. Daniel and his friends likely petitioned God for an approach that would allow them to remain set apart for his kingdom's purposes. Daniel's kingdom purposes were only enabled by seeking God's will and resolving to stand ready to act as God directs, into a path or future that seems similar to the resolution of avoiding defilement, which Daniel had plenty of. 

Psalm 1:7 says, “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and instruction.” King Solomon warns that acquiring wisdom requires effort and instruction. Proverbs states that one must first obtain the fear of the Lord and then follow certain conditions: “My son, if you accept my words and store up my commands within you, turning your ear to wisdom and applying your heart to understanding—indeed, if you call out for insight and cry aloud for understanding, and if you look for it as for silver and search for it as for hidden treasure, then you will understand the fear of the Lord and find the knowledge of God. For the Lord gives wisdom, and from his mouth come knowledge and understanding.” 

Notice, too, that the text says, “Now God had caused the official to show favor and sympathy to Daniel.” Have you ever requested that of God—for him to cause those over you to show favor and compassion to you? The text doesn’t say Daniel made that request, but he may have made it during his prayer. He certainly contributed to receiving the favor and compassion of Babylonian authorities by having already committed to God's kingdom purposes for his day. He was utterly incapable of accomplishing this alone, as are we. Daniel, in his weakness, let God work through him, and God must be involved in and through our weakness. At the very least, Daniel contributed to the Babylonian authorities’ favorable view of him by establishing a good relationship with his supervisors, obedience to their authority, and discipline in his work.

Without that obedience and relationship, it is unlikely he would have gotten an audience with Ashpenaz to appeal. Daniel never displays any animosity or revenge toward the Babylonian authorities that stripped him of his family, homeland, religion, and likely his ability to sire offspring. We discussed earlier how Daniel may have learned to navigate the challenges he faced. While God naturally graced Daniel with deliverance from such hardships, it seems more likely that the process he went through involved wrestling with his feelings and recovering before making this appeal.

Third, Daniel made his appeal in concert with his three friends, which suggests a great deal of coordination on their part, and thus, the strength of their collective resolve. Daniel made no demands of his superiors; instead, he thoughtfully offered a genuine alternative to the king’s orders to the chief official. He determined that he needed to avoid defiling himself, whether because he believed that requesting a vegetable diet alone could help him and his friends be “set apart” from the other captives entering the king’s service, or perhaps both at the same time. Was the king’s order for those entering his service intended to shame and pollute them? This seems unlikely, as he would be polluting everyone in his service. Instead, the Babylonian court offers the choicest food and drink, not generally available to the average Babylonian, to ensure the health and success of those entering the king’s service and to confer some status on his servants. The king wants people to succeed in gaining help to administer the empire. Daniel's alternative would accomplish the objectives of the king’s order to make the servants healthy. In fact, at the end of the trial period of ten days, they were said to “look healthier and better nourished” than the others.

The appeal Daniel makes also allows his alternative to be tested publicly. Daniel believed God would sovereignly help him in this test, and his dependence on God was demonstrable. He now puts his trust in the sovereign God to the test, not in rebellion to political control but in reliance on God alone. The test was not subjective either. He didn’t ask for a biased opinion; he encouraged the official to see whether the alternative Daniel proposed worked and evaluate the results by comparing his three friends against the others in the king's service. The fear of the chief official was evident. Daniel likely respected the commander and understood that the king expected complete obedience from his servants. Failure to carry out the king’s orders would likely result in punishment. By easing the official’s fear of failing to carry out the king's commands, Daniel accomplished his own purpose and that of the king's.

From these observations about his request, we can infer that Daniel made a righteous appeal in an appealing and righteous manner. Yet, Daniel didn’t shame the shamans, even though they worshipped wooden idols that could not speak and practiced a profession that offered no lasting value. While the Babylonians were famous for developing astronomy, it remained subordinate to their astrology. The whole cadre of magicians, astrologers, enchanters, and sorcerers was a caste of professional religious people who did everything from reading the entrails of dead animals to anticipating success or failure on the battlefield and interpreting the omens of human lives based on the positions of stars. They also included magic. Historian Will Durant explains some of these practices.

Daniel consistently displayed respectful obedience to political authority and never criticized the unrighteous practices of the Babylonians, even though such practices were outlawed for Israel in the Mosaic law. Magic formulas for the elimination of demons, the avoidance of evil, and the prevision of the future constitute the largest category in the Babylonian writings found in the library of Ashurbanipal. Some of the tablets are manuals of astrology; others are lists of seers. In Babylonian society, religion, administration, art, and warfare were intertwined and subordinated to their religious worldview to a degree we cannot imagine today. Leaders relied on their advisors for all significant decisions. This was true for the Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, and later the Romans, who relied on their readings of events, particularly war decisions. The decision to go to war was particularly fraught with uncertainty, leading to considerable use of divination, looking to the gods for information that human resources could not provide. As a result, individuals who could correctly advise on these actions were highly valued and could expect rich rewards from the kings they served.

Perhaps Daniel pitied the Babylonians for their religious beliefs. Maybe he, like Paul at the Areopagus, understood that pagan religious beliefs, though of human origin, may reflect a profound yearning for something more powerful than what life offers. He understood what Paul recognized about the pagans of his era: they were certainly closer to the truth than they realized. The ancient peoples and their religions reflected a view of life that was often bleak. One scholar pointed out that the account of their religion might suggest a people serene in the cultivation of their fertile land, but we must not forget the great quantity of magic and divination they employed in an effort to foresee and influence fate. The Babylonians, like the Sumerians, had a belief system that was not associated with any views on the afterlife. The "land of the dead" was a dark place, where its inhabitants dragged out a wretched existence, drinking dirty water and eating dust. No wonder Daniel didn’t shame them. He trusted God and knew he was fortunate to be alive and to serve in the court of Babylon. Daniel could execute God's kingdom purposes in an imperfect political kingdom. He didn't expect righteous behavior from people who didn’t know God.

Finally, Daniel diligently applied himself to his appeal. Early in the appeal, the Babylonian court looked for several qualities in its recruits for the king’s service. They had to be young men without any physical defect, handsome, showing aptitude for every kind of learning, well-informed, quick to understand, and qualified to serve in the king's palace. In addition to being perfect physical specimens and objectively handsome by the culture’s standards, the promising recruits had to pass tests to determine their intellectual aptitude for serving as advisors in the king’s court. Today, we use various measures to enter colleges, graduate schools, medical schools, law schools, and even the military. What tests the Babylonians employed, we may never know. It seems likely that the candidates were asked a series of questions about their understanding of the world, how well they grasped instruction, and their potential to serve in various positions of administration. We know more about the language and literature of the Babylonians than about the tests they used to assess aptitude.

The Babylonians preserved their predecessors’ traditions, culture, and religion. The Chaldeans made remarkable developments in astronomy and provided significant political figures during the Babylonian Captivity. Some of the art on the gates of Babylon was remarkable. Later, the Akkadians developed cuneiform writing on dried clay tablets, initially designed for commercial purposes, which eventually became the official language for diplomacy, scholarship, and religion. During the mid-eighth century BC, the Assyrian Tiglath-Pileser II introduced Aramaic as the lingua franca of the empire, which also became the common language of those subordinate to the Assyrian Empire. Eastern Aramaic, the language throughout the region, differed from the Palestinian dialect of Judea, but it was relatively easy to learn. So, Daniel and his friends had to learn the dialect of eastern Aramaic, Akkadian cuneiform, and likely Sumerian. Learning languages takes a great deal of effort. The modern Defense Language Institute trains military personnel in foreign languages, which may help guide us. A friend of mine studied in an intensive program for thirteen months to become fluent in Russian. While studying Russian, students also learn about Russian culture, history, customs, and practices. At the end of the thirteen months of intensive training, students are tested on a five-point scale for their proficiency. Typically, the military trains its students not so much to interact with others in their culture but to staff listening posts worldwide to understand what others are saying and thinking. After thirteen months of intensive training, my friend could interpret the Russian military language but was not fluent enough to speak Russian as a native. He would likely have been rated four to five in listening and three in conversation.

Daniel and his friends likely became very proficient at being scribes with Sumerian and cuneiform, and at least Daniel became fluent in Eastern Aramaic, as half of his book was written in it. In addition to literature and languages, our young friends likely also learned about administering an empire. Because Babylon sees little rain but sits astride the two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, the Babylonians kept detailed records of floods and irrigation. They also kept records on diplomacy and war. They recorded their architectural achievements, especially under Nebuchadnezzar, who began a vast building campaign that astounded the ancient world. Daniel and his friends would likely have been required to acquaint themselves with this rich tradition. They also had to learn Babylonian science and mathematics. As recently as 747 BC, the Chaldeans became more prominent in Babylon’s political history when Nabonassar, a Chaldean, ascended the throne. By then, the Chaldeans had established highly accurate records of the times and motions of the stars, so much so that they began to keep very accurate records for the various lines of kings. Later, the Greeks based their astronomy on the Chaldean understanding, which depended upon learning mathematics. All of their science, however, was subordinate to their religious understanding. It has been said that the Chaldeans, in particular, and Babylonians, in general, looked at the world to discover how things worked with religious undertones. In their records of the motions of the stars and the seasons, they also included detailed lists of floods, plagues, diseases, and other phenomena. They began to accumulate detailed descriptions of what animal activities meant for them—for example, what might happen when a dog peered into the temple or sat down by a king and what those actions portended for the future. 

It's almost impossible to know what Daniel thought about Babylonian religion because it was so intimately tied up with the Babylonian understanding of the world, politics, and administration. Yet we know he and his friends remained faithful to God while studying and gaining mastery over the beneficial aspects of their education. These young men worked extremely hard at their studies. They were at a ripe age, too, for modern neuroscience shows that from age twelve to twenty-four, the adolescent brain is pruning and growing new neurons at an accelerated rate in preparation for the rest of life. The brain at that age undergoes a radical and rapid transformation. Their diligent studying paid off. At the end of their three-year graduate program in Babylonian administration, our friends passed with flying colors. When evaluated by Nebuchadnezzar or an appointed servant, they were found to be an order of magnitude better than the magicians and the enchanters. God certainly equipped them, for in these four young men, God gave knowledge and understanding of all kinds of literature and learning. This is where God and humans intersect when, by diligent obedience to Him, God holds up those who excel in the tasks He gives them. 

At this young age, Daniel presented his appeal with less ego and more expertise than most of us do most of the time. Would that we practiced this conversational strategy with others in our day-to-day living. By seeking permission from Ashpenaz, Daniel asserts his needs while respecting the needs of others—in this case, the chief of eunuchs. Further, he does not say the Babylonians will defile him; he says he does not want to defile himself with the king’s choice of food. Daniel expertly phrased his appeal in another way. He never demanded success, nor did he demand changes in others. He let Ashpenaz determine whether the diet worked. After talking to Ashpenaz, Daniel submitted the proposal to the guard appointed over him and his friends. The guard was likely given guidance to allow Daniel’s proposed diet and to evaluate the results. We do not know whether the guard or Ashpenaz kept the extra portions of the king’s rations for themselves, which indeed would have been a temptation for either or both. Daniel submitted to the legitimate political authority of Babylonian officials throughout this process. While he determined that the best way to remain set apart for God's kingdom purposes in Babylon was to appeal respectfully, he also made it clear that he would not defile himself. It is also true that he likely would have been willing to die for his request, which should cause us to pause and reflect on how important the request was to Daniel. He wanted to have some separation from the political society but not a separation that would shame members of that society. At the same time, it seems likely that Daniel wouldn't hold a grudge against anyone in political authority should the appeal fail. Daniel refused to live with anger and bitterness toward the Babylonians despite all they had done to him. 

Daniel's exemplary life amid political events serves as a model for us today. Because he had committed to this first appeal, his benevolent approach to the dilemma should encourage us to follow his example. Daniel’s dilemma is our dilemma: how to obey political authority while avoiding becoming absorbed by that authority. God established political authority after the fall to restrain evil expression or allow for the rise of tyrannies. We are obliged to remain obedient and respectful. However, political authority is a concession to human sin. It is essential, but not supremely important, to the believer. God's kingdom transcends human political systems. Daniel wisely navigated the dilemma of obedience without absorption into the political order. He lived for God's kingdom first while remaining obedient to human authority. In his choice, he would remain dependent on God for his survival or be willing to die to stay faithful. 

Quite a few books have been published lately that use “The Daniel Diet” as the basis for healthy living. However, after reading a few, most did not evaluate what Daniel may have eaten. They may have taken their cues from the English translation of the words in the Bible but not from any records of Babylon. That is not to say that such books are worthless; many offer sound advice on healthy eating and living. But Daniel’s diet was a little different for him than what the books model. The average Babylonian rarely ate meat, and the poor could not afford it. Babylon, however, was the agricultural breadbasket of the ancient world. Scholars have determined from cuneiform records what kinds of foods the Babylonians consumed. They made unleavened bread, and barley provided the raw ingredients for beer, which only the upper-class people would have had regularly with their meals. The date palm featured prominently in Babylonian diets. The Babylonians also harvested pulses, primarily chickpeas, almonds, and pistachios from the hills. They would have had a variety of vegetables such as onions, leeks, turnips, lettuce, and cucumbers. Pomegranates were the most common fruits, along with dates and plums. They would have had a variety of foods such as apples, figs, and pears, along with dishes that included cress, mustard, cumin, and seeds. 

There are several key things to note about this diet. It would have consisted of unprocessed foods, which are often referred to as a healthy diet. Second, these foods would have been high in protein and contain appropriate fats. It would have been very high in soluble and insoluble fibers. More importantly, this diet would have been rich in nutrition, including vitamins and minerals, promoting good digestive health, which is vital for a young person entering the years in which their brains would be undergoing profound changes. A third thing to note is that there would be profound health advantages from such a diet. Allium vegetables, including leeks, garlic, and onion, have antibacterial and antiviral properties. Barley is packed with antioxidants, fiber, and vitamins. Almonds are also suitable for brain health and protein. Many of these foods would have had high concentrations of antioxidants. Combined, this diet would have been conducive to longer life expectancy with far lower risks of coronary heart disease, cardiovascular disease, certain kinds of cancer, and other chronic diseases. Based on what we know from Babylonian records and paleobotanical specimens, Daniel's diet would have been extremely good. His diet would have been very similar to the list of seven species outlined in Deuteronomy for the new land overflowing with abundant resources, consisting of wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and date honey. All of these, except olives, were produced in Babylon and were likely familiar to a religious Jew. Olives and olive oil would have been imported. No wonder Daniel served as long as he did in Babylon and the first year of the Persian Empire. He would likely have been in his eighties by then.

Daniel’s success with his first appeal would lead to further success with his second. Upon closer inspection, it appears that the chapters overlap. We know from history that Daniel served in the Babylonian administration during the period from 605 to 601 BC. He continued to serve Babylon until the first year of King Cyrus, which indicates that Daniel and his friends entered service during this time. What transpires reveals there are good reasons why, at the end of the first chapter, Nebuchadnezzar finds Daniel and his friends far better in knowledge and wisdom than the magicians and enchanters of his whole realm at their graduation in 602. In his second regnal year, the king directly observed this young foreign student’s wisdom and godly character. He saw Daniel successfully interpret the king’s troubling dream and compared this success to the manifest failure of all the other wise men in the face of the king’s wrath and commands. Only Daniel had intended to fulfill the king’s wishes, whereas the others sought to find a way out of the dilemma posed by the king.

During the second year of his reign, Nebuchadnezzar had a series of dreams, likely nightmares, that caused him to lose sleep. We don't know how many dreams he had or for how long they persisted, but they troubled the king. Dreams and visions were significant in the religion of the Babylonians and could portend major successes or failures. He needed to understand the meaning of his dreams. Because this dream deeply troubled him, he summoned Babylon’s professional wise men to help him understand it. The wise men, magicians, enchanters, sorcerers, and astrologers fully subordinated their education and learning to their religious beliefs. They were recruited and trained under Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar's father, and served in his court for a long time, so the king’s son would have been familiar with them. These wise men may have wanted to coach Nebuchadnezzar regarding how administration was done in Babylon.

The text describes Nebuchadnezzar’s summons to the wise men of Babylon. From the second chapter, the book is written in Aramaic, as these passages indicate. The astrologers answer the king with, “May the king live forever!” and then query the king about the dream. However, Nebuchadnezzar does not reveal the content of the dream to the wise men; instead, he insists that they tell him both the content and the interpretation. Did Nebuchadnezzar doubt their competence to advise him? The text does not let on. However, after their initial response, he says that he has decided that they must first tell him the content of the dream, or he will have them killed and their homes destroyed. They answer again a second time that they need the king to tell them the dream's content before they would interpret it. Nebuchadnezzar becomes convinced that these wise men are stalling for time, perhaps conspiring against him. He tells them there can be only one result should they fail to comply with his request.

While the wise men are not a bureaucracy in the modern sense, they tend to behave like one. Though they are composed of disparate people with different skills, they unite to constrain the king's request to something manageable for them. In their third reply, they inadvertently and unknowingly set the stage for God to intervene dramatically on behalf of Daniel in the unfolding drama. The astrologers answer the king, “There is no one on earth who can do what the king asks!” They emphasize their point: “No king, however great and mighty, has ever asked such a thing of any magician, enchanter, or astrologer.” They add, “What the king asks is too difficult.” Their response infuriates King Nebuchadnezzar, and he flies into a rage by ordering the execution of all the wise men of Babylon.

The text gives us insight into what happens next. When Arioch, the commander of the king's guard, went out to put to death the wise men of Babylon, Daniel spoke to him and asked why the king had issued such a harsh decree. Arioch then explained the matter to Daniel. At this, Daniel went in to the king and requested time so that he might interpret the dream for him. Then Daniel returned to his house and explained the matter to his friends Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. He urged them to plead for mercy from the God of heaven concerning the mystery so that he and his friends might not be executed with the rest of the wise men of Babylon. During the night, the mystery was revealed to Daniel in a vision. Then Daniel praised the God of heaven and said:

“Praise be to the name of God forever and ever; wisdom and power are his. He changes times and seasons; he deposes kings and raises up others. He gives wisdom to the wise and knowledge to the discerning. He reveals deep and hidden things; he knows what lies in darkness, and light dwells with him.”

I thank and praise you, God of my ancestors. You have given me wisdom and power. You have made known to me what we asked of you. You have made known to us the dream. Then Daniel went to Arioch, whom the king had appointed to execute the wise men of Babylon, and said to him, "Take me to the king, and I will interpret his dream for him." Arioch took Daniel to the king at once and said, "I have found a man among the exiles from Judah who can tell the king what his dream means."

The king asked Daniel (also called Belteshazzar), "Are you able to tell me what I saw in my dream and interpret it?" Daniel replied, "No wise man, enchanter, magician, or diviner can explain to the king the mystery he has asked about, but there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries. He has shown King Nebuchadnezzar what will happen in days to come. Your dream and the visions that passed through your mind as you lay on your bed are these:

As you were lying there, O king, your mind turned to things to come, and the revealer of mysteries showed you what is going to happen. Now we will interpret it to the king. Some historical background will help us here. Nabopolassar, a Chaldean, seized the throne of Babylon in 625 BC and struggled until 612. It took nearly a decade for Nabopolassar to gain control in Babylon. The Assyrians had ruled Babylon for a very long time and were unwilling to give it up without help. The Medes represented a relatively new power in the Mesopotamian area. In 614, Nabopolassar signed a treaty with the Median king, Cyaxares, and arranged a marriage between his son Nebuchadnezzar and the Median king’s granddaughter Amyitis. In 612, a coalition of Babylonians, Medes, and Scythians laid siege to Nineveh, the last stronghold of the Assyrian Empire. After three months, the city fell. The remnant of the Assyrians moved to southern Turkey until the Babylonians established a garrison there in 610 BC. The Babylonians defeated the small army at Carchemish in the spring and summer of 605 and defeated the remnants on both sides. The historian Matt Waters argues that we are to move from Assyrian descriptions of the Medes as dependent city-lords to the Medes as a unified force that was able to unleash significant military action against Assyria. Throughout their early history, the Medes sought to secure essential trade routes and were able horsemen. It could be that after so much time, the independent Medes began uniting against another possible foe to the east, the Scythians. After defeating Assyria, the Medes left Babylon alone while they ventured north into modern Turkey to conquer Urartu and Lydia. However, Nebuchadnezzar may have had some forebodings about Median ambition late in his reign, as he built a great defensive wall, the Median Wall, which ran from Sippar to Opis to keep out barbarian tribes and to make attacks from the north and east more difficult. Certainly, the Medes would play an important role in the eventual destruction of Babylon in 539. Could his dream have portended Median ascendancy over Babylon?

The Medes figured prominently in earlier prophecies. We see Isaiah, who wrote about one hundred years before our story, depict them, and Jeremiah, a near contemporary of Daniel, also mentions them in his prophecies. In all cases, the prophecies pertain to the eventual destruction of Babylon. While Babylon was not a great power during the early prophecies, it was an essential outpost of the Assyrian Empire. Some critics of the Bible chide the prophets for incorrectly identifying the Medes as the power that will eventually defeat Babylon. But to the Jews at the time, the Medes were the power to behold. The Persians were a minor Iranian people and not a power until the rise of Cyrus the Great around 550 BC. But the two became so close that it’s impossible to distinguish them by then. Cyrus himself was likely half Mede and half Persian. The Medes played an important role in the rise of the Persian Empire and the eventual defeat of Babylon by the end of Daniel’s time in political office. Ashpenaz, one of the court officials, also features in this story.

Daniel appeals to Ashpenaz not to appoint an anonymous guard over him. We never learn the guard's name, but he must have communicated with Ashpenaz in some fashion. After making his appeal to Ashpenaz, Daniel asks this anonymous guard to test the quality of the proposed alternative diet. We never learn whether the guard or Ashpenaz judged the quality of Daniel’s alternative diet, though the text implies it was the guard himself. 

In Daniel 2, we meet a new official positioned hierarchically somewhere between Ashpenaz and this anonymous guard. He is Arioch, the commander of the guards. Not much is told about him in the text, but it seems likely that Daniel may have developed a good reputation with both the guard and Ashpenaz. If so, that reputation would have spread to Arioch, who plays a vital role in Daniel’s second appeal. During the crisis of the king's epic anger outburst and the subsequent efforts to manage it, Arioch gives considerable latitude to Daniel to appeal for more time and clemency. 

Several things stand out about this appeal as well. As in his earlier appeal, Daniel demonstrates wisdom and patience beyond his years. Because of his first appeal, he likely developed an excellent rapport with the people he worked with. But he moves and speaks with humility, intending to solve the king’s problem. Wisdom and respect seek solutions for problems that have no clear-cut answers. Daniel and his friends continued the excellent rapport they had begun earlier. Because they subordinated themselves to the political authority of Babylon, they gained great favor in the king’s service. And because of this, Daniel had a chance to ask his question. Given that the decree was so harsh and urgent, Daniel wisely asks why it was so. 

Arioch explains the situation to Daniel. Daniel does something else to prepare for his second appeal: he seeks out all the facts of the situation. Instead of listening to second or third-hand information about the king’s decision to execute all the wise men, Daniel sought out Arioch, the person tasked with implementing the order. He ignores the gossip and seeks the truth. Daniel and his friends are a praying community of believers. After explaining the situation to his three friends, he requests them specifically for an answer to the king’s problem, not for the king to change his mind or for God to soften his heart. He understands that the king has a real problem, or at least what he perceives as a real problem. He urges them to plead for God's mercy for an answer so that they will not be executed along with the other wise men of Babylon. 

To advance politically and bureaucratically, lower members of a political apparatus will frequently align themselves with the careers of senior people for protection and opportunities. Mentors are expected to help mentees advance in their careers with the reciprocal expectation that junior partners will support the aspirations of their mentors. Although they are in the king’s service, Daniel and his friends don't seek out a patron to protect them. Perhaps because they were out of the protection of a patron within the kingdom, Daniel nonetheless made friends through his wisdom. However, nothing in the text indicates that he relied on human authority for advancement and protection. 

Notice that in his appeal, Daniel never challenges the harshness of the king’s order. Daniel lived with the knowledge that political authority given to kings and governments includes the ability to use lethal force, whether against internal or external enemies. This does not mean every decision to use force is thereby right in God's eyes. Daniel never debates whether Nebuchadnezzar's decision was just or not. Was it because he believed the king was just? Did he realize he could not challenge the king's authority? Or do we see Daniel behaving wisely under the sovereign constraints of his circumstances? 

We see examples of Daniel throughout the book, living under extreme conditions. As part of his appeal, Daniel then goes to see the king and asks for a little more time so that he might interpret the dream for him. Did he actually speak with the king? Does Arioch permit him to see the king? By all accounts, Daniel doesn’t enlist the help of Ashpenaz, the chief of the court officials, who helped Daniel with his first appeal. But Arioch must have known Daniel’s reputation through Ashpenaz and perhaps his guard. So, either Daniel went in on his own to see the king, or Arioch must have somehow had confidence in Daniel to allow him to seek a little more time from the king, perhaps hoping that Daniel could do what the professional wise men of Babylon were incapable of doing. 

Daniel was likely granted little time, maybe only twenty-four hours, to solve the king’s problem. As in the interpretation of the dream, the king, or Arioch on behalf of the king, would likely have seen Daniel's new appeal as credible because of his reputation. Daniel makes several appeals in his request. In addition to appealing to the king, Daniel appeals to his friends to pray for mercy from the God of heaven and asks them to join him in doing so. His friends could have said no. Alongside them, he appeals to God to answer his request. 

Daniel speaks to Arioch not to execute the wise men on behalf of the king, which Arioch could have declined. Daniel finally seeks to present the results of God’s answer to the king's concerns by requesting an audience to share his understanding of the dream. Given how poorly the professional wise men behaved, Nebuchadnezzar could have also declined. At the very least, the king was deeply unsettled. We don’t know if there was something disarming about Daniel's approach. 

What makes an appeal so appealing? An appeal signifies a request for something. But demands are less likely to elicit cooperation. It is weaker to ask or protest based on our weaknesses rather than on our strengths. Daniel understands the mystery provided by God. When he speaks of the kingdom of men in the Bible, while Daniel acknowledges God’s sovereign rule, at least from reading Scripture, the revelation must have increased his understanding with greater clarity than ever before. He not only understood God's sovereign constraints on his life but now fully grasped God’s sovereign constraints on political leaders in history and into the future. How much confidence would that have given him to pursue righteous political living?

He also uses the occasion to praise God before the king without pushiness or self-righteousness. In perfect timing, the king asks Daniel, “Are you able to tell me what I saw in my dream and interpret it?” The wise men had only recently said, “There is no one on earth who can do what the king asks!” They further added that “no one can reveal it to the king except the gods, and they do not live among men.” Daniel agrees with the sentiment of the wise men by saying, “No wise man, enchanter, magician, or diviner can explain to the king the mystery he has asked about, but there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries.” He takes the focus off of himself, off the wise men, and off the false gods of Babylon, and puts the focus and praise squarely on the living God of heaven and history. Daniel makes it clear that he correctly answered the king because they did not have the insight that comes from the living God. 

Daniel goes further and spreads the request to his friends. After hearing from Arioch about the king's decree, he requests that they pray to escape the impending execution. The text indicates nothing about Daniel critiquing or criticizing the religion of the wise men or the priestly caste’s ranks, behavior, or corruption that was likely endemic in Babylon. He was undoubtedly aware of what Nebuchadnezzar was saying about them. However, Daniel likely had some inkling of God’s sovereignty. Daniel continues to respect and never challenge the political authority of the king. He appropriately uses the chain of command by going to the correct person, Arioch. He doesn’t try to jump over the commander charged with carrying out the king's command by going to Ashpenaz or someone else. 

Further, in this appeal, he only asks Arioch why the king’s decree was so harsh. The Aramaic word for harsh here can mean either “harsh” or “urgent,” a term that other translations use instead of harsh. Urgency certainly seems more appropriate to the context of the text, as the king commissioned Arioch to collect all the wise men of Babylon for execution. It’s not hard to imagine Arioch having to catch his breath to hear Daniel’s question. Arioch or the king likely only gave Daniel a very short time to petition God to solve this mystery. Daniel also avoids using any influence he may have had. Recall that Daniel appealed to Ashpenaz in his first request. He didn’t try to use his relationship with Ashpenaz to avoid the fate of the wise men. Nor does it appear he used any influence or patrons to help him with the problem he faced.

No, the only one Daniel relied on was the God of heaven. Throughout his second appeal, he continually sought permission by appealing to others to find out the reasons for the king’s decree, to get time from the king to pray for a solution to the mystery, to get his friends to join him in prayer to God, to seek God's wisdom on what the king dreamed and how to interpret it, and to approach the king with the resolution of the mystery. After God reveals the mystery to Daniel, wonderful things happen. Daniel approaches Arioch and asks that he stay the execution of the wise men and allow him to present the king with God’s answer. Arioch then takes Daniel to the king but tells him that he found someone among the Jewish exiles who can explain the dream. Daniel never contradicts Arioch and instead allows him to take the credit. It may sound small, but this is another hallmark of Daniel’s humility. He does not boast or take credit because the solution to the king’s nightmares is God’s doing, not his own. Further, he never lords it over the wise men that he solved the king’s dreams when they couldn't. There’s no trace anywhere of self-righteousness or self-aggrandizement from Daniel. 

As a young person, Daniel remained calm in the face of near-certain death by the king’s decree. Young people are neurologically more likely to react strongly in challenging, demanding, or extreme situations. Neuroscientists point out that the brain’s limbic system is more reactive to threats than the executive function of the brain. When we “flip our lid,” our survival mechanism reacts faster but can overpower longer-term reasoning. During adolescence, young people undergo rapid pruning and replacement in their brains. They tend to feel issues of right and wrong far more strongly than earlier or later in life. Because their limbic system fires intensely, as anyone with teenagers knows, the executive function can go offline for some time. Yet Daniel not only displays calmness in the face of threats to his life and that of his friends but also engages the executive functioning part of his brain. He expertly phrases his appeals to ensure the maximum possibility of success. 

Notice how, in his appeal, Daniel seeks out the facts of the matter while maintaining respect for authority and never questions the right of political authority to carry out the king’s decree. He seeks out information before he makes his decision to appeal for time. He relies on facts, not limbic-system-generated interpretations of the situation, and he doesn’t rely on second and third-hand information. Daniel seeks a little time to make the interpretation known when he appeals. This would have been tricky for him, given that Nebuchadnezzar believed the wise men were playing for time. But he makes it clear that the time he needs is not open-ended, nor is he engaging in subterfuge; he seeks to make the interpretation known. He strives to fulfill both the king’s request and the desire of the king. How refreshing would his approach be to the king, who had only recently expressed his desire for the wise men? The king now has someone who seeks to help him. 

Daniel never stepped outside the bounds of his execution of political authority. Even when he tells Arioch to find someone who could interpret the dream, he later lets Arioch take credit for finding someone. He never challenges political authority and rails against the king's urgent and harsh decree. He merely responds respectfully. He never gets self-righteous about his appeal, nor does he boast about his ability to interpret dreams. Nebuchadnezzar's mysterious dreams are a dilemma that Daniel gives credit for solving to the God of heaven. He praises God, who solved the dilemma for Daniel and answered his request for the interpretation of the dream. Daniel also credits Nebuchadnezzar, telling the king that God has shown him what will happen in days to come. To Daniel, God honored Nebuchadnezzar with the dream, and he should be grateful to the God of heaven for having them. He praises the king for being a worthy vessel of such vital information. 

Nebuchadnezzar honors Daniel at the end of the chapter. He orders that an offering and incense be given to Daniel and acknowledges that Daniel serves a mighty God. He says, “Surely your God is the God of gods and the Lord of kings and a revealer of mysteries, for you were able to reveal this mystery.” Then, at some point later, he promotes Daniel to administer the province of Babylon and puts him in charge of all the wise men. We are not given a time-stamp at the end of this chapter that indicates when this occurs, except that it is after the initial offering and incense. Since Daniel was still in his second year of schooling, and we know from the end of the previous chapter that the king evaluates Daniel and his Jewish colleagues as outstanding graduates, he must have been put into such a leadership position upon his graduation, sometime around 601 BC. Still, he would have been very young, around nineteen years old. 

Daniel also becomes more vulnerable to the bureaucratic machinations of the other wise men of Babylon. Critics of the book of Daniel usually do not care for chapters that are apocalyptic and reveal what is to come. They tend to dismiss these chapters as written later after the events, so of course, that doesn't solve all the issues. This is partly because Nebuchadnezzar’s dream of the statue is apocalyptic. It reveals different kingdoms, from Babylon and on into history to include something like our day. There will be a final kingdom, one not made by human hands, that smashes the last kingdom, the kingdom of iron and clay. This is understood to be the establishment of God’s kingdom on earth, which started when John the Baptist announced the kingdom was at hand and would be finalized at a later time. 

Daniel reveals that the image in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream was a giant statue of a man. On top was a head of gold. Below the head was a chest and arms of silver. The belly and thighs of the statue were of bronze. Its legs were of iron, and its feet were partly iron and clay. These metals were well known to the ancients of this time, as they are to us today, but the clay may have been something like the clay the Babylonians fired in furnaces to make bricks for their impressive building campaign. The furnace that Daniel’s three friends will be forced to endure may have been used to fire clay bricks. 

Daniel interprets the dream. The head of gold represents Nebuchadnezzar's political reign. Daniel mentions what makes the head of gold—Nebuchadnezzar’s reign—superior to the subsequent kingdoms. Daniel says, “Your majesty, you are the king of kings. The God of heaven has given you dominion and power and might and glory; in your hands, he has placed all mankind and the beasts of the field and the birds of the sky. Wherever they live, he has made you ruler over them all. You are that head of gold.” 

The silver of the chest and arms represents a new political order that will be “inferior to yours.” The silver represents the kingdom that began under the reign of Cyrus the Great. A third kingdom is understood to be the Macedonian (sometimes called Greek) Empire and is represented by bronze and “will rule over the whole earth.” The fourth kingdom, the Roman Empire, is represented by iron and its military force. The feet of clay mixed with iron are understood to be the kingdoms that arise from the Roman Empire, likely those that will rule until the time God's kingdom establishes itself. 

Daniel's interpretation may be puzzling in some ways. In what sense was Babylon superior to subsequent gentile empires? As we will see below, the land area under the sovereign control of the various empires depicted in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream starts to expand slowly (with Babylon), expands greatly under Cyrus, shrinks somewhat under Alexander the Great, and never achieves the same extent under Rome. The peak size of the empires suggests something interesting as well. Babylon's peak year of size was 562 BC, at the death of Nebuchadnezzar. Several more kings ruled until Persia conquered Babylon in 539 BC, but none had the vigor of the second king. The peak size for Persia was in 500 BC, about thirty years after the death of Cyrus, during the reign of his greatest successor, Darius. The peak size of the Macedonian Empire was in 323 BC, at the death of Alexander. His heirs divided the kingdom into four areas, two of which subsequently emerged in the Macedonian world in the West. Rome was at its peak in AD 117, nearly fifty years after the founding of the Roman Empire in Jerusalem and many years after the events described in the interpretation. This, along with something Daniel suggests, indicates that the coherence and greatness of these human kingdoms had to be acknowledged. That is to say, it has affected the effectiveness of political rule during these times.

Nebuchadnezzar ruled over his kingdom with almost absolute efficiency, while other rulers governed their empires less effectively. Cyrus and others had varying degrees of authority. Nebuchadnezzar was able to exercise complete sovereignty, as indicated in the biblical text, where he could change the laws of the Medes and Persians. Alexander the Great's real genius was in military conquest, but he did not live very long after he conquered his empire. While still an empire, Rome had to contend with more and more checks on the emperors’ power. Over time, the relative power of all kingdoms depicted in this dream diminishes until other human kingdoms conquer them. Note that the statue in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream also ignores other powerful states and empires throughout history. During the peak years of Roman rule, when Rome had a land area of some 1.93 million square miles, Han China had over 2.5 million square miles. Subsequently, other empires have come to occupy much greater land areas, including China, Russia, Canada, Brazil, and Australia. The central focus of the statue, however, is on the nature of human, gentile kingdoms. It is evident that people throughout history can expect trouble from these kingdoms, which, however regarded by human standards of power and greatness, pursue the rulers’ interests, not God's. They never pursue God’s interests except incidentally, when God directs their hearts, as we have seen before. And, as the biblical texts make clear, these rulers fight the Lord himself and his followers. Daniel interprets the dream to mean the weakness of human kingdoms. The mixture of strength and weakness suggests that they will have a hard time maintaining their unity. The toes were partly strong and partly brittle, indicating that this kingdom will be partly strong and partly weak. Just as you saw, the people will be a mixture and will not hold together, as strong does not mix with weak. The author suggests that this time will be far more brittle than during the reign of Nebuchadnezzar and even of the subsequent kingdoms. Daniel’s interpretation of the apocalyptic dream seems to suggest that it will become more complex and more challenging for gentile regimes to hold on to their legitimacy and authority, even while they appear to be ruthlessly strong toward their citizens. We see something like this from time to time today. The Soviet Union, which looked powerful in the 1970s and 1980s, crumbled quickly once the political leadership lost its legitimacy. Like dominoes, a whole series of subordinate nations followed when Soviet legitimacy was called into question. China has elements of both the harshness of power and the fragility of legitimacy, as was on full display during the Tiananmen crisis. 

In the biblical narrative, we read about Daniel's political authorities. He remained politically subordinate yet used his wisdom to make appeals to his friends and political authorities, even in a hostile political system. He found a way to flourish in an environment that may not have been conducive to his beliefs by remaining committed to God's kingdom purposes. The year 539 BC brought tumult for Daniel. He recorded significant events that year that would transform him, his fellow Jews, international politics, and the kingdom of God he served. That year finds Daniel serving in high political office, first in the Babylonian Empire's final days and then in the Persian Empire's early days, which are events foreshadowed in the biblical texts. Daniel was called out by the ruling faction of Belshazzar’s political officers to interpret some strange handwriting on the wall. The evening of the party where Daniel announced the reading of the handwriting foretold the imminent fall of Babylon and Persia's conquest of it. Babylon did fall, changing the regional political structures of Mesopotamia and the Middle East. The new king, Darius, appointed Daniel to one of the three top administrative posts in the new Babylonian situation, now under the control of the Persian Empire. Over several months, Daniel’s political opponents conspired to discredit him in the eyes of the new king. Their trap elicits Daniel’s faithful conduct. Daniel discovered a passage from Jeremiah that spoke of the seventy years of exile. Jeremiah had prophesied that Judah would be made captives for the persistent rebellion of the whole society. By the way Daniel writes, he seems to have discovered this prophecy for the first time. Jeremiah’s prophecies were sent to Babylon, where Daniel could have taken them. For example, one of Jeremiah's letters encourages the Jewish exiles who live in Babylon to prosper by serving Babylon. Daniel understands from reading the book of Jeremiah that the terminus for the seventy years of exile is drawing close. From another biblical passage, we learn that Cyrus gave the edict to restore Babylon's captives to their original homeland in 538 BC. Historically, some Jewish exiles began their return that year, but the full return didn't happen for many years. The passage hints at the reluctance of some to return. To determine the end of the seventy years of exile, we must determine when the exile began. Was it 605 BC with the deportation of Daniel and his three friends? If so, then only sixty-seven years would have passed later, with the largest deportation occurring in 586, when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed the temple. Several hypotheses have been suggested regarding the different dates for Jeremiah’s prophecy. The first is that the seventy years began in 605, while the largest deportation didn’t occur until 586 with the destruction of the temple. Daniel’s journey reflects the complexities of faith and politics, as he navigates the challenges of his time while remaining committed to God's purposes.

The destruction of the first temple occurred in 586 BC, and the second temple was completed in 516 BC. This timeline is crucial for understanding Daniel's context. The first hypothesis regarding the exile suggests that it begins with Josiah, which aligns with the seventy years until Cyrus issues the edict. However, no clear reason is provided for this specific timeline. Another explanation may be found in the biblical text. The prophecy was likely written between 609 and 605 BC, after the death of the last righteous king, Josiah. This period coincides with when Nebuchadnezzar began his conquests. In this passage, the prophet laments the robbery and oppression of the common people in Judah by the nobles. The prophet states: “How long, O Lord, must I call for help, but you do not listen? Or cry out to you, ‘Violence!’ but you do not save? Why do you make me look at injustice? Why do you tolerate wrongdoing? Destruction and violence are before me; there is strife, and conflict abounds. Therefore the law is paralyzed, and justice never prevails. The wicked hem in the righteous, so that justice is perverted.”

A prominent Old Testament scholar notes that the lament addresses the corrupt and rapacious nobles allied with corrupt religious leaders who used the courts to oppress and rob the poor. It was the nobility that was first taken into captivity during the preliminary deportations of 605 and 597 BC. The majority of the lower classes remained in the land until the third deportation. Daniel's reading of Jeremiah’s prophecy compelled him to seek restoration for the exiles back to Judah. He understood the relationship between God and Israel from the Mosaic covenant, which lays out the consequences of obedience and disobedience. It is not merely a decree but a loving encouragement for Israel to return to God with all their hearts and to live well with Him. There are blessings for obedience and consequences for disobedience, including removal from the land to allow it to rest. Recall that ten of the original twelve tribes had already been destroyed by Assyria over a hundred years prior. The remnant of Judah and Benjamin was the last group of people God called out from the human race. God jealously guards His promises. The final section of Leviticus offers a way to return to obedience and blessing: “But if they will confess their sins and the sins of their ancestors— their unfaithfulness and their hostility toward me, which made me hostile toward them so that I sent them into the land of their enemies—then when their uncircumcised hearts are humbled and they pay for their sin, I will remember my covenant with Jacob and my covenant with Isaac and my covenant with Abraham, and I will remember the land. For the land will be deserted by them and will enjoy its sabbaths while it lies desolate without them. They will pay for their sins because they rejected my laws and abhorred my decrees. Yet in spite of this, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them or abhor them so as to destroy them completely, breaking my covenant with them.

I am the Lord their God. But for their sake, I will remember the covenant with their ancestors whom I brought out of Egypt in the sight of the nations to be their God. I am the Lord.

Daniel's prayer of repentance and restoration aligns closely with the Levitical passage. He confesses his sins, the sins of his ancestors, and the sins of his fellow Jews. He acknowledges the rebellion and iniquity that led God to discipline His people through military conquest and deportation. Daniel humbly appeals to God's mercy for deliverance, reminiscent of how God delivered Israel from Egypt centuries before. Daniel likely also considered Solomon's temple dedication and the Levitical laws. In the dedication, Solomon lists the conditions for prayer for repentance and restoration. It is important to remember that Babylon would destroy Solomon's temple in 586 BC. In this context, Solomon stands before the Lord, God of Israel, and acknowledges, “There is no God like you in heaven above or on earth below—you who keep your covenant of love with your servants who continue wholeheartedly in your way.”

Daniel's prayer reflects a deep understanding of the covenant relationship between God and His people, emphasizing the need for repentance and the hope for restoration. He expresses humility, saying, “How less this temple I have built! Yet give attention to your servant's prayer and his plea for mercy, Lord my God. Hear the cry and the prayer that your servant is praying in your presence this day. May your eyes be open toward this temple night and day, this place of which you said, ‘My Name shall be there,’ so that you will hear the prayer your servant prays toward this place. Hear the supplication of your servant and of your people Israel when they pray toward this place. Hear from heaven, your dwelling place, and when you hear, forgive.”

If they sin and you become angry with them and give them over to their enemies, who take them captive to their own land, far away or near; and if they have a change of heart in the land where they are held captive, and repent and plead with you in the land of their captors and say, “We have sinned, we have done wrong,” and pray to you toward the land you gave their ancestors, toward the city you have chosen and the temple I have built for your Name; then from heaven, your dwelling place, hear their prayer and their plea, and uphold their cause. And forgive your people who have sinned against you; forgive all the offenses they have committed against you, and cause their captors to show them mercy; for they are your people and your inheritance, whom you brought out of Egypt, out of that iron-smelting furnace. May your eyes be open to your servant's plea and to the plea of your people Israel, and may you listen to them whenever they cry out to you. For you singled them out from all the nations of the world to be your own inheritance, just as you declared through your servant Moses when you brought our ancestors out of Egypt.

Comparing the passages of dedication, all three appeal to God, who brought His people out of the land of Egypt. God had first given Israel His inheritance out of all the nations of the earth. Later, God delivered His people, who had been enslaved for over four hundred years by the superpower of the day. Solomon’s temple and Daniel's prayer may prove helpful. All three passages state or imply several things in common and some unique things. However, only Leviticus explicitly states that God will remember the remnant of His people even under extreme conditions, which could include captivity. Daniel, in his prayer, acknowledges that there would be no hope for a prayer of repentance and a remnant if they were to leave God. Solomon suggests that God's people should pray toward Jerusalem in general or the temple in particular. But Solomon emphasizes the importance of praying toward the temple when they have been defeated by an enemy and toward Jerusalem and the temple when God's people are held captive in foreign lands. Solomon knows too well that God could discipline rebellious Israelites through conquest and enslavement by gentile nations. Solomon’s injunction likely explains why Daniel made it his practice to open the western windows of his apartment and pray three times a day toward Jerusalem and the desolate sanctuary. There was no requirement in the Mosaic law to do so. Still, Solomon makes it a critical point in his dedication. Solomon and Daniel understood that God doesn’t reside in His temple but in heaven, where He answers prayers.

The term "covenant of love" denotes the relationship God first established with His chosen people. All gentile nations rebelled against God at the Tower of Babel by trying to make a name for themselves instead of relying on the power of God's Name. Because of their rebellion, He disinherited them by scattering them to be ruled over by foreign gods. Only then does God call forth His portion from among the nations. Daniel had a deep understanding of the laws and requirements. He lived in light of the Scriptures he had access to and learned from them, leading a life of faith and trust in God. Early in his career, he committed to his small circle of righteous friends, supporting each other with prayer and petitions for one another and the challenges they would face. The prayers of his three friends helped him discern the nature and interpretation of Nebuchadnezzar’s dream, which confounded the king's court magicians. Daniel and his friends excelled at the king’s service, vastly outperforming the other elite children Babylon had taken captive. Daniel was known for his righteousness and right living. He lived in such a way as to glorify God and highlight God's sovereign rule from the heavens among gentile kings and officials, even those who despise God. In his time in Babylon and early Persia, the angel Gabriel calls him “highly esteemed.” Ezekiel elevates Daniel alongside Noah and Job as among the three most righteous people living. Daniel stayed alert to God’s sovereign movement through history. He challenges us to be prepared to understand how God sovereignly moves throughout our history in our country and the world. While Daniel had the prophecies of Jeremiah and Isaiah to guide him during his day, few of his contemporaries paid nearly as close attention to them. We, too, have Scriptures that are helpful for us today. Our core mission is found in the Great Commission—to make disciples throughout the world and reverse the disinheritance of the gentiles that began after the Tower of Babel. The question remains: Are we staying ready to serve God today through our righteous living and study of Scripture? We see the gospel spreading worldwide, and our mission remains an unfinished task. Can we discern how our days intertwine wisely to help or hinder the spread of the gospel? Are we investing wisely in the spread of the gospel?

The region known as the 10/40 window refers to North Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia, where the gospel has had the least impact. In general mission, the specific mission for each of us will differ. While that mission has placed us in unique settings, serving in high offices does not make us special. However, God has sovereign purposes for each office in foreign lands. We are to take agency here and now. God’s kingdom purposes are revealed for a variety of reasons. He intercedes on behalf of his fellow Jews in Babylon. The Hebrew word for prayer has many forms, and several Psalms reference it. Prayer is an appeal to God for assistance, usually in acknowledgment of previous acts of kindness, compassion, and righteousness. Daniel’s supplications are numerous throughout his prayer. He implores God to listen, forgive, and act. Daniel indicates that he also fasts and covers himself with sackcloth and ashes while appealing to God. Everyone has sinned; they have missed the mark of the way of God in their dealings with Him and with others. They did this by doing wrong or committing iniquity. Daniel also appeals to God because his people and desolate sanctuary bear His name. He can only fall on the mercy of God. Daniel continued throughout his life in Babylon to remain faithful to all political and divine authority. As indicated above, God answered Daniel’s prayer very quickly. Cyrus announced the mandate to allow people to return home. There are several alternative degrees of certainty regarding this. Still, we know that it did not only take the prayer to move, or was God waiting? Daniel receives another answer, one he could not have anticipated. As his prayer and fast conclude, the angel Gabriel interrupts him at the evening sacrifice to give him new information about what is to come for his people. Seventy "sevens" are decreed for your people to finish transgression, to put an end to sin, to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal up vision and prophecy, and to anoint the Most Holy Place. Know and understand this: From the issuing of the decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem until the Anointed One comes, there will be seven "sevens" and sixty-two "sevens." The Anointed One will be put to death and will have nothing. The people of the ruler who will come will destroy the city and the sanctuary. In the middle of the "seven," he will put an end to sacrifice and offering. And at the temple, he will set up an abomination that causes desolation until the end that is decreed is poured out on him. By the grammar of the text, it looks like there is a total of seventy "sevens," understood by conservative Christian and Jewish scholars as seventy weeks of years, equaling a total of 490 years. These seventy "sevens" are divided into three groups: the first seven "sevens" of forty-nine years, the sixty-two "sevens" of 434 years, and the final seven of seven years. There are three conservative Christian schools of thought about this revelation. For lack of better terms, I have labeled them the preterist, messianic, and messianic plus tribulation schools. The preterist view holds that because most Jews did not repent of their sin and rebel against God near the end of the Babylonian captivity, the captivity was extended to seventy weeks of years. This view holds that the end of these 490 years coincides with the zenith of Israel's independence under the Hasmonean kings. The more traditional school of thought (messianic plus tribulation) is that the 483 years lead up to the birth, ministry, and death of Jesus the Messiah on behalf of humanity and the subsequent destruction of Jerusalem and the temple. The remaining week of seven years occurs during end times when the tribulation, the final seven years of human history before the millennial reign of Christ, occurs. The messianic view, which is a less traditional but still conservative school, holds that the final week of seven years is when the Anointed One will be put to death and have nothing, referring to the sacrificial death of Jesus Christ on the Cross and the subsequent destruction of Jerusalem that followed. While it may be disconcerting that, even with hindsight, conservative scholarship cannot fix the interpretation of Daniel’s seventy weeks of years, the more profound message had its intended effect. There is strong historical evidence that different Jewish schools of thought believed the messiah would come sometime during Jesus’ ministry. These schools included the Essenes, the Pharisees, and the Zealots. The intended message of hope for the coming of the messiah, the solution for the problem of sin, and the inauguration of the kingdom of God happened within the timeframe of expectations that Gabriel's revelation of the seventy weeks of years would have occurred. Only when he did not come, in Jewish thinking, did these schools reinterpret the prophecy as non-messianic. Christian thinkers picked up the messianic interpretation where Jewish scholars left off. Interestingly, however, all interpretations of Daniel’s seventy weeks have something in common. They are remarkably detailed in their understanding of the prophecy. We will see how this plays out with Daniel’s final appeal in the next chapter, where he appeals for greater clarity on the next installment of the apocalyptic revelation he will soon receive. 

We are now at a place where we assess the reliability of the book of Daniel. Scholars today hold to one of two main hypotheses about the book. Either a single author in the late sixth century BC wrote the book, or it was written during the second century BC by an anonymous Jew; the purpose of the book, according to these latter scholars, was to encourage the Jews during the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who persecuted the Jews, especially for their faith. Judaism has traditionally confirmed the former thesis since before the time of Christ, and orthodox Christianity has done so since its inception. The latter took hold after the Enlightenment in the West. Many recent scholars hold to the second-century hypothesis for various reasons. Most, though not all, argue that the book, or significant parts of it, were written during the second century to encourage the Jews when their faith was being tested by that diabolical king. For this reason, this approach is called the late-date—or Maccabean—hypothesis. It has been argued that the book was penned during the Maccabean revolt against the Seleucid Empire.

Most scholars who support this view argue that the apocalypses, or revelations, were written after the fact, meaning that Daniel's prophetic accuracy up to the time of Antiochus IV could only be explained if the anonymous author wrote it during that particular king’s reign. In other words, the book of Daniel was not prophetic, and thus, his prophecies of the future are also considered false. Scholars from both sides of the debate present evidence to support their positions. Internal evidence comes from the book itself or from other parts of the Bible, while external evidence comes from history, archaeology, or written sources. We will test the arguments and evaluate which hypotheses may be more reliable or coherent. We will also evaluate some unstated assumptions both sides hold. Assumptions frequently direct our inquiry and guide our conclusions, which may be unclear. Such assumptions can allow us to connect the dots or distort disconfirming evidence. They may cause us to ignore or downplay evidence and arguments. For all of us, it is vital to assess the various assumptions before evaluating the hypotheses. This also includes determining which hypothesis has the least disconfirming evidence. The hypothesis that the author of the book was a single person situated in the late sixth century BC has the least disconfirming evidence and the most confirming evidence for it. Given this, I assess that it is almost certainly true that a single author, probably Daniel, wrote the book sometime in the late sixth century BC. The hypothesis that an anonymous Jew wrote the book of Daniel during the second century BC has the most disconfirming and least confirming arguments and evidence in its favor. Consequently, I assess that there is almost no chance that it is accurate or the best explanation of the book’s authorship. Arguing for the best explanation leads a dispassionate reader to accept the authorship of the book of Daniel. Four main sub-hypotheses support this assessment:

There is overwhelming evidence that Daniel was recognized, by name, as the book’s author by a contemporary in Babylon, subsequent Jewish scholars, several New Testament authors, a non-Christian Jewish Roman historian, and the early church fathers. Jesus acknowledged Daniel as the book’s author. The book exhibits coherence and a point of view that makes it likely the work of a single author from the late sixth century BC. The author's use of various languages, including Hebrew, Aramaic, Persian, and Greek, points to a single author in the late sixth century BC. Knowledge of critical people, religion, and details of court life in Babylon would unlikely have been known with such detail after the fifth century BC—at least until modern archaeology dating from the nineteenth century AD was available. This sub-hypothesis focuses on how early Jews and Christians—Jesus included—viewed the book's authorship. A near contemporary of Daniel, the prophet Ezekiel, refers to him twice in his book, probably published about thirty years after Daniel established his career and reputation in the court of Babylon. Human sources of information are considered more reliable when proven trustworthy over time. Ezekiel had this in spades. He was likely taken in the second wave of exiles around 597 BC, about seven to eight years after Daniel, who was taken in the first wave around 605 BC, and would have been intimately familiar with Daniel’s growing reputation. Ezekiel praises Daniel for his wisdom and ability to solve mysteries. Critics argue that the Daniel listed with Noah and Job in Ezekiel is someone other than the traditional subject of the book. However, it is far more likely that the prophet referred to the contemporary, exiled Jew, who, like him, had established a reputation for wisdom and righteousness in Babylon. Contemporary Jews in exile would know of Daniel from his actions in Babylon rather than from a myth acknowledged by any other book in the Old Testament. Every part of Daniel's book was preserved in the Dead Sea Scrolls of Qumran, which existed around 150 BC. It is highly unlikely that the community at Qumran would have regarded the book of Daniel as authentic had it been recently written or portions of it written by an anonymous Jew living near the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Jewish scholars had a test for the reliability of prophets and prophecy, which had been laid out in Deuteronomy: “If what a prophet proclaims in the name of the Lord does not take place or come true, that is a message the Lord has not spoken. The prophet has spoken presumptuously, so do not be alarmed.” As Jewish scholars noted, Daniel's prophetic accuracy until Antiochus Epiphanes was outstanding. The book of Daniel and other Old Testament books are part of the Tanakh in Hebrew. The Tanakh went through a process of canonization.

Scholars believe prophecy ceased in the question of the book of Daniel’s authorship. The book is categorized under the Hagiographa rather than the Prophets. According to Josephus, the Septuagint, and the Talmud, it is indicated in Jewish circles that the Prophets and Hagiographa are significant. All thirty-nine Old Testament books are the same as the twenty-four books of the Tanakh. All were considered to be inspired by God and canonical. In the Talmudic tradition, the book is regarded as authoritative for religious practice, and its authority is binding upon the Jewish people. Such books are to be studied and expounded upon. Talmudic scholars examined whether it was composed under divine inspiration, that is to say, whether God spoke the words or whether He gave the words to the prophets. According to Jewish scholarship, Hebrew Scriptures in Jewish and Christian traditions were considered inspired and canonical. Some Jewish literature may be regarded as canonical but uninspired, but not the Tanakh, our Old Testament. For example, the Mishnah, which contains exegetical material, judgments, and rulings on the law by Talmudic scholars, is considered canonical but uninspired in Jewish tradition. Scholars considered the book of Daniel to be inspired and canonical. By the first century AD, if not before, Talmudic scholars considered all of the books of the Tanakh as canonical. There remains some uncertainty about when the canonization may have happened, as it may have occurred in stages. A fair reading of Daniel suggests that the books were already considered canonical when Jesus, the apostles, and the early church fathers affirmed the book of Daniel as inspired and canonical. New Testament authors also affirmed the canonicity and inspiration of Daniel. The New Testament understanding of inspiration is similar to the Jewish understanding. This makes sense as the early converts were Jewish. In 2 Timothy, the apostle Paul declares, “All Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting, and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work.” “God-breathed” is a literal translation of the Greek word “theopneustos,” from which we get our term “inspiration.” God actively breathed His ideas to and through the writers of the Holy Scriptures without changing their personalities, vocabulary, or idioms. The apostle Peter makes a similar claim in 2 Peter: “Above all, you must understand that no prophecy of Scripture came about by the prophet’s own interpretation. For prophecy never had its origin in the human will, but prophets, though human, spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.” Human will cannot prompt God’s inspiration. Christians applied this maxim along with other criteria to determine the inspiration and canonicity of the Old and New Testament works, and the book of Daniel has always been regarded as among them. The Apostle John in Revelation also regarded the book of Daniel as significant. The early church fathers accepted the book of Daniel. The term “canon,” from the Greek word meaning “rule” or “measuring stick,” refers to the standard by which we determine authenticity. The church fathers who regarded Daniel as canonical include Melito, Origen of Alexandria, Epiphanius, and Jerome, among others. An early critic was Porphyry, a third-century philosopher with a personal agenda against Christianity. The book of Daniel exhibits a coherent point of view that likely makes it the work of a single author from the sixth century. A quick reminder about the structure of the book of Daniel: the first six chapters are considered Babylonian and Persian court narratives, while the last six chapters are apocalypses or revelations. However, that is only part of the picture, as Daniel documents King Nebuchadnezzar’s revelation of future kingdoms that only Daniel can understand. About half of the book is written in Hebrew, and the other half is written in Aramaic.

The simple division of the book into Hebrew narrative and apocalyptic chapters belies the complex organization. The book displays a chiastic structure, where chapters two to seven form an outer parallel with the apocalyptic chapter. A chiasm is a literary device in which parallel arrangements reflect a central theme. Chapters two and seven form an outer parallel in which Nebuchadnezzar dreams of four earthly kingdoms and a fifth eternal kingdom. The core of the book emphasizes the succession of ancient Gentile kingdoms and the latter days when the Antichrist will arise. In addition to Daniel’s chiastic structure for chapters two to seven, he crafts chapter seven as the linchpin between the book’s narrative sections and the Hebrew language apocalypses. Chapters one to twelve are primarily set during Daniel's lifetime, but chapters seven to twelve provide revelations of the future. Chapters one to six focus on God’s sovereign rule over Gentile empires, while chapters seven to twelve focus on Daniel's visions of the future rule of Gentile empires and the suffering believers can expect until the Messiah sets up an eternal kingdom on earth. Chapter seven belongs to both sections, reiterating the succession of ancient Gentile kingdoms while providing more detail about the latter days. The structure of the book amplifies the horrific persecution the Jews will face under Gentile kingdoms, up to and including the end times. 

In summary, the five appeals Daniel makes are as follows: 

First, Daniel appeals to the commander of officials to choose a vegetarian diet to avoid defiling himself. Second, Daniel appeals to Arioch, the captain of the king’s bodyguards, to seek God’s help in revealing Nebuchadnezzar’s dreams. Third, Daniel makes an appeal without words to King Darius, who was tricked by his sub-officials into framing Daniel for violating a political order. Fourth, Daniel appeals to God for the forgiveness of Israel’s sins and is met by an angel, Gabriel, who gives him further information on the future of Israel. The author of the book uses ancient Aramaic and Hebrew for distinction, borrowing available loan words where appropriate. Daniel wrote this book in two different languages for two distinct audiences. The sections written in Aramaic were intended for a wider audience of Jews and Gentiles living in Babylon, while the Hebrew portion was written with Jewish concerns in mind, such as the future of Judah under Gentile kingdoms—concerns of less interest to Gentiles themselves. 

In one instance, the prophet is told that parts of the text were to be closed and sealed up for a yet future age, which are the sections that give skeptics the most pause. Daniel also uses foreign loan words, including three Greek words for musical instruments and fifteen Persian words for politics and administration. Skeptics of Daniel’s authorship claim that his use of Greek words places his writing during the second century BC, during the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes. They argue this because Palestine had been Hellenized from the time of Alexander the Great. However, Greek culture and language had passed into the Levant and even to Mesopotamia by the tenth century BC, several hundred years before the book of Daniel was written. Greek words for musical instruments would have easily been available throughout the Near East, especially with the large numbers of Greek mercenaries used by different empires in Mesopotamia before the rise of Babylon. 

Importantly, contrary to critics, the work during the second century BC would not have included the specific political terms used in Palestine for more than six centuries. Even more importantly, Daniel's knowledge of critical people, religion, and details of court life in Babylon was unlikely to have been known with such detail after the fifth century BC—at least until modern archaeology dating from the nineteenth century AD. Only an author in the late sixth century BC would have known the intimate details of the Babylonian Empire's court life and its critical personnel. Historians have known that Nabonidus was the last king of the Babylonian Empire. Until discoveries of cuneiform tablets referring to Belshazzar as "the son of the king" were made, critics pointed to this as a historical error in Daniel. Belshazzar was appointed as regent in his father’s place while Nabonidus went out to subdue his opponents. Daniel correctly observed that Belshazzar only promised him to be promoted to third ruler in the kingdom, as Belshazzar was second to his father. 

Even after the tablets established Daniel’s trustworthiness, some critics still argue that Belshazzar was not Nebuchadnezzar’s son but Nabonidus’s son. However, such criticism fails to acknowledge that the ancients frequently referred to sons as successors in office, which has been observed in Jewish and Egyptian sources contemporary with the time of Daniel. Late-date proponents cast doubt on the authorship of Daniel, suggesting that the book was written to encourage Jews during a horrible time of persecution under Antiochus Epiphanes. However, the author depicts Nebuchadnezzar favorably, which raises questions about the intent of the text. It remains unclear how the narrative would have encouraged the Jewish people if it were meant to refer to Antiochus, who was overthrown by the Jewish revolt against the Seleucids, leading to the Maccabean period of Jewish history. 

Traditional scholars argue that Antiochus Epiphanes serves as a type, a form of prophecy that foreshadows what is to come. We are familiar with spoken prophecy, where the prophet tells the reader what can be expected. Typology is an unspoken prophecy that foreshadows what will come. Types of prophecies are used throughout the Bible. The first man, Adam, foreshadows Jesus Christ, according to the apostle Paul. Their faith was in a future king. Antiochus Epiphanes foreshadows the end times and the Antichrist, who will eventually die in Jerusalem. The final wicked ruler, Antiochus Epiphanes, in one of the prophecies foreshadows world history before the return of Jesus Christ, a kingdom that will destroy all human kingdoms. There were two "little horns" in Daniel's prophecies, one from the third kingdom (Antiochus IV Epiphanes) and one from the fourth kingdom (the Roman Empire). 

Daniel also related many prophecies for which the late-date authorship hypothesis cannot account. Several stand out as they relate to events that have occurred in history since the second century BC but before the end times. The first is that in both chapters two and seven, Daniel points to the emergence of the fourth empire of Rome. Though Rome had been around for some time and had established a vassal protectorate in Egypt, it only took control over the Levant in 63 BC. Second, as we saw in his fourth appeal, Daniel was given a prophecy of the coming of the Messiah, which indicated that sometime during his ministry, he would be put to death. The events mentioned by Daniel were still in the future and were not the work of Antiochus. Finally, Daniel predicts the destruction of the temple after the Messiah was cut off, which eventually occurred in AD 70. Many of Daniel’s other prophecies remain for the future or toward the end times. But, as required, Daniel’s history and short-term prophecies must first be proven true. Only after that can we trust his long-term, yet-to-be-fulfilled prophecies. 

However, just as the prophecies of the past serve as a type, Daniel's end-times prophecies are ripe for understanding. The words in the book of Daniel will not be fully understood until the time is right, and the latent information has been preserved to help the righteous understand what unfolds before their eyes. We are at a point where conducting a key assumptions check becomes essential. Each position must be evaluated to determine if the assumptions cancel each other out. One hypothesis assumes that a transcendent God created the entire universe. Therefore, prophecy is possible but not likely. Another hypothesis believes that prophecy is impossible or a variation on it: there is no God, and that contradicts the assumption of prophecy’s truthfulness. It is, therefore, incumbent on us to evaluate the lines of evidence for each hypothesis. Did a single author write the book in the late sixth century BC, or did an anonymous Jew write it in the second century BC? 

As we have seen, there are fewer disconfirming lines of evidence favoring the late authorship hypothesis, suggesting that the book was written by a single author in the late sixth century BC. On the other hand, there are far more disconfirming items of evidence for the second-century authorship hypothesis. This strongly suggests that the assumptions, not the evidence, drive the second-century authorship hypothesis.

Advocates for this latter view would be well-served by explicitly stating their assumptions and revising their interpretation of the book’s authorship accordingly. The Key Assumptions Check lets us know whether the evidence or our explicit or implicit assumptions drive the analysis. In our case, the results strongly suggest that Daniel authored the book in the sixth century. Just because the book of Daniel is difficult does not make it untrue. On the contrary, there is much to commend us to believe it is reliably accurate.

In my exploration of the subjects surrounding Daniel's life, dreams, visions, prophecies, and the authenticity of his work, I identify at least seven different types of books. These works generally stem from one of two distinct philosophical assumptions: either spiritual matters are real and trustworthy, or such matters are mythical or unreal. Historically, the majority of people have aligned with the first perspective, while the latter view has gained traction primarily in the last several hundred years, particularly in the West. This shift in perspective has permeated academia and, consequently, society at large.

Those who affirm the reality of spiritual matters include many pastors and theologians who have commented on the book of Daniel, often reflecting their specific theological traditions. This group also encompasses authors who provide expository Bible studies that delve into Daniel's life and his faithfulness in Babylon. Additionally, Daniel is the subject of numerous children's books that highlight his courageous faith, particularly in the lion's den and during the fiery furnace incident involving his three friends, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. Furthermore, some authors focus on the eschatological themes present in Daniel, as the book contains more apocalyptic passages than any other text in the Old Testament.

A significant number of authors defend the reliability of the book of Daniel, asserting that it is inspired by God and trustworthy in its accounts. These authors believe in the reliability and authenticity of the Christian and Jewish ideas surrounding the inspiration of Scripture, which affirm that God guided the writing of the text. Conversely, many authors dispute the authenticity and reliability of the book of Daniel. These critics often adopt a philosophical worldview of naturalism, which posits that only those things that can be physically proven are considered reliable. This group primarily consists of secular scholars, although some liberal theologians and non-scholars may also align with this perspective. Many of these critics view the book of Daniel as a work of historical fiction, while others argue that some parts may be fictional while others could be factual.

A newer wave of writers approaches the subject from a postmodern perspective. Postmodernism, emerging in the late twentieth century, generally rejects the modernist assumptions that have developed since the Enlightenment. While there are various types of postmodernism, it is characterized by skepticism, subjectivity, and a suspicion of objective reality. Postmodernists often question the role of ideology in maintaining political power and express doubt about the concept of truth. In their view, questions of authorship are often seen as unimportant, focusing instead on the audience's interpretation rather than the author's intent.

In this manuscript, I evaluate the book of Daniel using abductive reasoning, which involves reasoning toward the best hypothesis or explanation. This method will help modern readers understand the nuances of what the author records. I will explore whether the book of Daniel can be considered reliable and credible. Specifically, I will address whether an authentic and credible individual named Daniel, who lived in Babylon in the sixth century BC, wrote the book based on historical events. I will also consider whether the book provides credible testimony to remarkable events and prophecies recorded in Babylon, or if it was written by an anonymous Jew during the second century BC, rendering it a work of historical fiction. These questions are crucial, especially given the significant scrutiny the book has faced since the Enlightenment and particularly since the nineteenth century.

I will also use abductive reasoning to explain various nuances within the text. For instance, I will discuss why Daniel may have requested a restrictive diet of vegetables and why he did not resist the pagan name Belteshazzar given to him by court officials. Additionally, I will explore how Daniel may have avoided the trauma of forced captivity, a common experience throughout history, and how divine assistance may have played a role in this. I will examine how Daniel made wise appeals throughout his career, demonstrating mature approaches to complex situations without clear solutions. He exemplifies the ancient biblical virtue of wisdom, seeking balanced and respectful resolutions to specific dilemmas. Daniel's life offers exceptional examples of living wisely in tumultuous political times. Although our era differs from his, the challenges of politics remain constant, and lessons from his life can inform our engagement with neighbors and governing authorities.

The foundational principles of political communities differ fundamentally from those of Christian and Jewish communities. In the former, the dynamics of politics are often rooted in power relationships, while the latter emphasizes grace, respect for others, prayer, and divine love. Daniel models engagement in politics that aligns with God's kingdom purposes, demonstrating a way to live faithfully within the political realms of Babylon and Persia while prioritizing God's kingdom. The power dynamics of politics reflect the human problem of sin. Since the fall, humanity has repeatedly betrayed God's best intentions for themselves and the world. From the early chapters of Genesis, we witness rampant evil, leading God to destroy most of humanity through the flood and later instituting capital punishment to restrain murder. However, after the flood, humans quickly established new political structures for ungodly purposes, culminating in the Tower of Babel, where they sought to bring God to them rather than fulfilling the dominion mandate. Consequently, God dispersed humanity, creating diverse political arrangements that still rely on power relationships between rulers and the ruled.

In Romans, the apostle Paul asserts that God appoints governing authorities to restrain evil and promote the common good. The Bible generally assumes that governments serve to protect societies better than political anarchy, which is defined as the absence of higher political authority. Throughout history, where political authority fails, anarchy has often led to the rise of warlords, gangs, and terrorism. While government is generally intended for the good of society, it remains a concession to human sinfulness. Sin is, in essence, a manifestation of worldliness. When dealing with politics, we confront a genuine form of worldliness. Because of this, and because of original sin, Christians may be tempted to believe that the only way to live politically is to use force to engage in it or else to retreat from politics altogether. Both options have storied histories in Christian tradition, including the extremes of the Crusades and monasticism.

On the other hand, Daniel evokes a subtler, more nuanced approach to politics. He was engaged but separate from the society he served. His approach may help us engage in politics more wisely in our day. Like Daniel, many people throughout history and worldwide have lived without the right to vote or publicly participate in the state’s political affairs. Such societies, though certainly not all, may be hostile to Christianity or expressions of Christianity that are not approved by a state-sanctioned view of religion. In more extreme cases, the state may persecute Christian beliefs and Christians themselves. In a way, this starkly simplifies the problems and choices Christians have, though not always. Many Christians today must negotiate life in hostile political conditions and are faced with the dilemma of whether, where, when, and how to consider obeying, confronting, or disobeying political authority. Daniel faced similar challenges and exemplifies what it was like to live without the right to voluntarily participate in politics yet do so with wisdom and respect for governing authorities.

In more democratic societies, with rights of speech and political engagement, there are different challenges. First, the prevailing culture of naturalism in democratic societies frequently demands that Christians keep their faith out of public discourse. It can feel oppressive to live in a society where Christians are the odd people out, like being on a playground with school bullies running the show.

It tempts us to bond closer to our Christian brothers and sisters, to those who hold our worldview, and to view and treat others as the enemy. Under these conditions, Christians sometimes seem to believe that a political right means a God-given license to say whatever they want in opposition to the dominant culture regarding their preferred policies, politicians, and politics. Because Daniel attained high rank in both the Babylonian and Persian eras, he exemplifies how to think about engaging in politics while exercising our political freedom with wisdom and respect. Daniel offers modern Christians an excellent model to consider when engaging with our political world. Our times are undoubtedly tumultuous. Whether we live in conditions of authoritarianism or political freedom, Daniel offers us clues about how to live wisely with an understanding of a transcendent reality wherever we live. Daniel clearly understood how to live with God's kingdom foremost in his mind while subordinating his physical and political life to it. The essence of this work is that Daniel led an exemplary life of faith during the historical political events of his day because he was committed to God's kingdom purposes for his time. Thus, Daniel models how we may live exemplary lives of faith by committing ourselves to God’s kingdom purposes for our day, even in our troubled political times.

Before exploring the book of Daniel and its meaning for how we live well politically, I want to address its credibility and reliability. Is the book a genuine work of sacred Scripture? Is the author of the book an ancient Jew by the name of Daniel? While questioning the book’s authenticity may seem sacrilegious to some believers, many critics openly deny its credibility or reliability. Since the Enlightenment, skeptics have derided or dismissed the book of Daniel as inauthentic because they claim it is the work of a pseudonymous Jew who wrote during the second century BC and, therefore, a work of historical fiction. Some go so far as to say that parts of the book may have been written during the sixth century and others during the second century, though they frequently disagree on which parts were written during which century. Most critics mainly believe that the book of Daniel, or significant parts of it, were written anonymously to encourage Jews during the traumatic reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who sought to “Hellenize” and persecute the Jews during his rule. Some in the current evangelical community seem to be adopting a version of this interpretation.

The challenges to the authenticity and reliability of the book of Daniel vary, but they should be addressed. The problem with the authors’ assessment is that they affirm a real Isaiah in the eighth century and the existence of Daniel in the sixth century, yet they could have given them specific information in advance. The authors suggest there could have been “prophetic schools” established later as well, without providing any evidence they did. There are several reasons why the book of Daniel confounds modern thinking. First, the author initially wrote the book in two different languages: Hebrew and Aramaic. Second, the author employed two distinct literary genres: court narrative and apocalyptic revelations. The use of these separate genres does not correspond with the use of the two different languages. The book also includes visions given to the author and a king, prayers, and a narrative of deliverance for some people. Lastly, apocalyptic literature—which includes both prophecies and apocalypse—likely troubles modern people because, if true, it betrays the existence of something supernatural.

Traditionally, God commissioned prophets to be His spokesmen to warn His people and nearby peoples of impending discipline when they strayed. Modern scholars hold to a paradigm of naturalism with a fairly rigid consensus. This consensus has grown steadily since the Enlightenment. Those who explicitly hold to naturalism believe that the universe contains everything needed for life to flourish. In a kind of physical determinism, some will go so far as to say that they can explain the entirety of physical phenomena from natural processes, including how we came to think and behave the way we do. Even our very thoughts may result from our chemical processes or genetic inheritance. Thus, the modern consensus eliminates God from the equation of history and processes. Therefore, when it comes to a book such as Daniel, the naturalist will likely assume that prophecy is impossible, that God does not exist, or that, if God does exist, He remains unknowable. Theists, on the other hand, may or may not accept the reliability of prophecy. Yet, if they do, they may still dismiss its validity by arguing that humans frequently lie and work to serve their own interests. Others may believe that some myths, though false, can still benefit people of faith. These varying approaches and perspectives lead to different arguments about the book of Daniel.

There is value in approaching the two primary propositions about the reliability of the book of Daniel as an intelligence problem. An intelligence problem requires an analyst to marshal the evidence, arguments, and assumptions to evaluate and support an assessment of which explanation or narrative makes the most sense of the problem. Every day, thousands of government analysts worldwide try to make sense of indicators of what other nations and people are doing or intending to do. Analysts must process vast amounts of data and information that flood them daily. Over thirty years ago, it was commonplace to say that the amount of information that U.S. intelligence agencies received could fill the entire Library of Congress every twenty-four hours. With the advent of the internet and now the emerging Internet of Things, the scale of information to be addressed has increased by multiple orders of magnitude. Because of this, intelligence analysts now rely on data science, or meta-analysis, to keep abreast of the big picture while still attending to the smaller points of data individually.

However, trying to make sense of all the data and information involves a more complicated process than we may think. The information and data in intelligence agencies are often incomplete, ambiguous, contradictory, or at least at odds with other bits of information. This increase in information may add to the burden on the analyst trying to make sense of it without providing greater clarity. In regard to the complex problem of data and information management, analysts, like all of us, also hold beliefs or assumptions they may be unaware of and make inferences based on those assumptions. Do facts speak for themselves? For example, imagine a man lying in the gutter. Stop and ask yourself, what’s the first thing that comes to mind? When two people are presented with the same fact, something interesting occurs: they may infer different things about the same fact, likely based on an assumption of which they may not be consciously aware. These inferences may depend on what is salient to the hearer.

Every psychological model gives researchers several tools to help us understand the powers and limitations of human reasoning. Jervis highlights many things in his research, but several ideas stand out. First, people tend to be theory-driven more than data-driven in their thinking. Rather than attend to every fact every time, we create efficient ways of thinking about things that focus on some facts and disregard others. As we go through our education and socialization process, we develop theories or beliefs about what constitutes acceptable behavior and how and why different people and nations act the way they do. We selectively attend to or ignore information to construct our experience, as humans can only make a limited number of discriminations on any stimulus continuum, whether colors or sounds, and can retain only that magic number of items in our short-term conscious memory. We also assimilate new information into our existing beliefs. People will attend to alternative explanations until they have decided on an issue. However, once formed, our beliefs become highly resistant to change. People can usually interpret evidence to support their opinions—an experience everyone has had with someone who refuses to change their mind, regardless of the evidence. Because of this, we tend to see what we expect to see, which is both a cognitive benefit and a limitation. We benefit from this efficient way of thinking.

For example, I hold to a theory that my wife loves me. So, when I come home late at night after teaching a seminar at the university and she appears grumpy, I don’t infer that I did something wrong. I infer she must have had a bad day. It's rational to continue believing she loves me unless she gripes at me for three consecutive months and I don’t pay attention. Mindsets, beliefs, and behaviors tend to be quick to form but resistant to change. This tendency is seen in our natural habit of creating the first reasonable hypothesis about how things work and how people behave. Scholars call this tendency “satisficing,” a neologism for satisfying. Unless one thoughtfully considers alternative explanations, they may fall into the trap of “premature cognitive closure,” which leads them to look for evidence confirming their initial hypothesis and ignore evidence contradicting it. Such cognitive strengths and limitations need to be accounted for in the analysis. Intelligence agencies have developed various analytical tools to address these limitations. These tools or techniques are methodologies that bring greater integrity to the analysis. Over the last thirty or more years, intelligence agencies have continually worked to improve their craft, providing analysts with more and better analytical tools to evaluate the problems they seek to address. One such technique, called an Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, is useful to assess the reliability and credibility of different views on a subject. An Analysis of Competing Hypotheses helps us compare a set of mutually exclusive explanations or hypotheses. It involves identifying the various hypotheses, the items of evidence that support or contradict each one, and the implicit or explicit assumptions behind them. The Analysis of Competing Hypotheses process will make those implicit assumptions explicit and test whether these drive the assessment of the hypotheses or whether the evidence drives the assessment. An illustrative sample of the Analysis of Competing Hypotheses matrix includes lines for evidence and arguments for and against each hypothesis. Analysts must assess the credibility of the evidence, as not all evidence is of equal value. The analyst must also consider the believability or reliability of the source of the evidence—whether it is testimonial, documentary, technical, or interpretive. Assumptions will be evaluated as well. Assumptions do not rely on credibility the way evidence does, but each assumption must be reasonable and justified by the person holding the belief. Additionally, assumptions should be made explicit. Unfortunately, many authors fail to do this. In the sample matrix, there are lines of evidence. I evaluate the credibility of the evidence using a system known as the Fuzzy Credibility Assessment. Under each hypothesis, a mark indicates whether that line of evidence supports or contradicts that hypothesis. In addition, intelligence analysts must assess the credibility of the evidence, and we will do so here as well. Not all evidence is of equal value because the analyst must also consider the believability or reliability of the source of the evidence—whether it is testimonial, documentary, technical, or interpretive. That is to say, if evidence contradicts all hypotheses, it ultimately explains nothing. If any individual is rejected as nondiagnostic, the evidence supports or determines the best explanation. Keep in mind that assumptions work differently than evidence. Evaluating whether something is true is based on assumptions, which are useful. The truth of an assumption is not at issue. In a particular international relations theory known as realism, theorists assume that nations are rational actors. Of course, there are many actors and agencies; nations often work at cross purposes. This assumption helps simplify the theory, even though we know that not all political actors are always rational. Abductive reasoning is a specific form of reasoning within the scientific method that includes deduction and induction. Deductive reasoning seeks to show that something is necessarily true and follows from the assumptions and axioms in the premises. Inductive reasoning, conversely, seeks to show that something is probably true by testing hypotheses against the available evidence, much of which may be generated by experimentation. Based on the available evidence, abductive reasoning tries to show what is possibly or most likely true. Most physical sciences rely on deductive reasoning, while the acceptability of the theory is empirically tested primarily through inductive methods. Most of the work in intelligence, on the other hand, relies on abductive reasoning, reasoning to the best hypothesis. Most people who argue for or against the authorship of the book of Daniel follow the deductive form. However, they only partially follow the scientific method in testing against the available evidence, as we only have minimal evidence on questions of historical reliability and cannot generate experiments to develop new evidence. Different groups, including evangelicals, theists, nontheists, and skeptics, hold to different theories or assumptions about what is possible, which tend to drive their search for evidence and explanations that confirm or disconfirm their hypotheses. Abductively assessing the available evidence, assumptions, and arguments helps us determine the best explanation. The Assyrian policy of relocating the best and brightest citizens of conquered lands helped prevent rebellions from conquered peoples. To avoid overcrowding and other problems, the Babylonians took only those they needed for specific projects. Over time, as available jobs, housing, and food supplies changed, the Jews who arrived over several migrations began to assimilate into Babylonian society. The first forced migration occurred in six hundred five BC when Nebuchadnezzar defeated Pharaoh Necho at Carchemish. It involved only a few people, one of whom was Daniel. In five hundred eighty-six BC, as a result of Judah's rebellion, Babylon destroyed the city of Jerusalem and the temple. No longer would Jews worship there. Trauma from being taken to a foreign land was significant. For those who suffered, it could be unbearable and almost impossible to cope. In Babylon, the boys were stripped of their identity by being given pagan names after the gods of Babylon. Observers have noted for some time the effect that wars can have on soldiers who have experienced wartime brutality, calling it “war neurosis,” “shell shock,” and “battle fatigue.” These observations have persisted throughout history across all cultures. However, it is not only war that causes trauma; interpersonal violence, child abuse, severe childhood neglect, sexual assault, and slavery can also induce trauma. Many psychologists, psychiatrists, and neurologists have been doing extraordinary work in understanding how trauma affects the minds, brains, and bodies of those who suffer from it. One of the leading researchers into trauma and PTSD, Bessel van der Kolk, has noted that those who suffer from PTSD are often trapped in the past and cannot find a way forward. It is the result of a fundamental reorganization of the central nervous system based on having experienced actual trauma. Being trapped in the past also has other effects. Daniel, and perhaps his friends, were royal descendants of Hezekiah’s lineage. Further, the Hebrew word translated as “court officials” is sarasim, the plural word for eunuchs, the same word commonly used among the ancient Assyrian and Babylonian Empires. More frequently than not, the practice of making men into eunuchs to serve in royal courts was prevalent. They would cut off the testicles or the penis, and sometimes both. Sadly, eunuchs were held in contempt by the lords over them, especially if they were conquered. Nevertheless, some of these eunuchs would rise in service to the king. The agony and cruelty of emasculation is hard to fathom, which may be why few commentators address the topic, or when they do, they gloss over it. It may also be why many translations substitute “court officials” as here. Scholars have paid little attention to the trauma of eunuchs in the ancient world, but we know that the experience in the court of kings was harsh. Imagine the trauma. Trauma and PTSD researchers have made significant advancements with the development of new scientific disciplines. We may find some insight from van der Kolk, who notes several ways therapists can help people overcome trauma. He suggests that each traumatized person may need one or more of these therapies to deal with the effects of their experience: top-down, by talking and reconnecting with others, and allowing themselves to know and understand what is going on inside them; by taking medicines that shut down inappropriate alarm reactions, or by utilizing other technologies that change the way the brain organizes information; and bottom-up, by allowing the body to have experiences that deeply and viscerally contradict the helplessness, rage, or collapse that result from trauma. The modern pharmacological approach would have been unavailable to Daniel and his friends. As captives, these four young friends likely had to walk a great distance back to Babylon. Walking has a potent therapeutic effect. One of the more effective treatments for PTSD and other forms of trauma was recently discovered by a research psychologist while taking a walk herself. In 1987, Francine Shapiro walked through a park, thinking about some distressing situations. She noticed that her rapid eye movements while walking greatly reduced the stress associated with those memories. She began to consider a new technique, now known as Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR). She then incorporated EMDR into her clinical practice. She discovered that it was a component of a much quicker and more effective treatment for helping trauma victims overcome their past. The treatment includes talking and eye movement. It has been so well researched since her discovery that the American Psychiatric Association, the US Department of Defense, and the Department of Veterans Affairs recommend it for treating trauma. There is a divine interest in having governing authorities, for the absence of such authority produces anarchy. Societies without political authority provide opportunities for criminal gangs and other elements to seize local control for their own ends, often not for the well-being of the public at large. Because God mandates political authority, people are commanded to pray for those in authority, whether or not they voted for those leaders. If you find it difficult to pray for the political authorities over you, look around the world today where failed states exist and see how warlords and criminal gangs manage the affairs of those societies. God sovereignly rules over the individual rulers of nations as well. God sets up and deposes kings at his pleasure. Moreover, God may direct a king’s heart to accomplish his earthly purposes. In the Lord’s hand, the king’s heart is a stream of water that he channels toward all who please him. Though a pagan, King Nebuchadnezzar was considered a servant of God, doing God’s bidding by defeating Judah and subjecting its citizens to Babylonian control. It was prophesied that another pagan king, King Cyrus of Persia, would accomplish what God intended for him to bring an end to Babylonian rule. This is what the Lord says to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I take hold of to subdue nations before him and to strip kings of their armor, to open doors before him so that gates will not be shut: I will go before you and will level the mountains; I will break down gates of bronze and cut through bars of iron. I will give you hidden treasures, riches stored in secret places. Through the prophet, God calls Cyrus God’s anointed and selects him to release the Jews to return to their homes in Palestine to rebuild the temple of Jerusalem after their Babylonian captivity. It is said, Cyrus is my shepherd and will accomplish all that I please; he will say of Jerusalem, ‘Let it be rebuilt,’ and of the temple, ‘Let its foundations be laid.’ In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, in order to fulfill the word of the Lord spoken by Jeremiah, the Lord moved the heart of Cyrus king of Persia to make a proclamation throughout his realm and to put it in writing. God's knowledge encompasses all, as expressed: You have searched me, Lord, and you know me. You know when I sit and when I rise; you perceive my thoughts from afar. You discern my going out and my lying down; you are familiar with all my ways. Before a word is on my tongue, Lord, you know it completely. God's hand is upon us, constraining us individually for his purposes. King David marveled at the breadth of God's knowledge and power compared to humanity. When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is mankind that you are mindful of them, human beings that you care for them? God mindfully thinks of us. Individually, we are constrained by God’s sovereign control of all the levels above us. God's purposes in his kingdom constrain international politics. God remains sovereign; he decides and establishes the laws of human behavior. We humans frequently try to circumvent these laws while avoiding the consequences of violating them. At a minimum, God provides humans with rational minds that can link cause and effect, detect truth, and see correlations. Our logical minds were initially given to us as part of our creation in the image of God to help build God’s dominion on earth. With the ability to reason, we can make inferences, such as when we view creation and infer there must be a powerful and good creator. We can also understand human nature and the role and purpose of moral laws, which enhance the human and social condition. Political philosophers and church leaders have regarded these as natural laws, common-sense laws inferred from studying how human societies work best. In the book of Romans, it is outlined what happens to believers and unbelievers who violate the laws of rationality. It shows the trajectory of unbelievers toward increasingly irrational thinking as they pursue unrighteousness to suppress the known truth of God revealed in nature and their conscience. God is not the Eye of Sauron from a well-known tale, unblinkingly staring at each person, waiting to harm him or her; instead, the wrath of God is expressed through their increasingly irrational thinking and behavior, almost a law of soul physics for human beings. God expresses wrath through the consequences of increasing irrationality for those who suppress the truth in the pursuit of unrighteousness. It could be said that God expresses his wrath on those who violate a fundamental law of physics, like gravity, by trying to step out into thin air, believing they may avoid the consequence of falling downward. Believers, on the other hand, face a different problem. In attempting to follow the call of God, believers frequently fail to remain humble about their ability and the desires of their hearts. Believers are reminded that they have been given the grace of God, both the desire and the ability to act according to his will. Just as importantly, it is pointed out that when we judge others, we often reflect our own hearts, for we have likely thought of the very sins and failures we presume to see in others.

Positively, Jesus encourages his followers to treat others as they would like to be treated. This principle sums up the Law and the Prophets. Do you want people to think kindly of you? Then think kindly of others first. Give people the benefit of the doubt when you see something that seems odd or out of place. In a community of believers, if our behavior were descriptive of our society, it would look vastly different today. While we are hemmed in by God, He allows us to choose to follow Him or our folly. The Western mindset often overestimates the importance of free will. As we've seen, there’s a great deal over which we have no control. The Scriptures tell us we need God’s help to incline our hearts, even as we incline our ears to wisdom and apply our hearts to His words. There is, therefore, a balance between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. 

Daniel lived in that balance. He was forcibly taken captive out of his homeland and marched by military convoy to Babylon, with only his three friends as companions. Babylon likely took captives from the surrounding provinces of Palestine outside of Judah, so Daniel must have been among a variety of people, probably with few connections to him. Once in Babylon, he was likely castrated as part of the practices of the Near East at the time and given a pagan name, distancing him from his ethnic identity and rendering him incapable of fulfilling the requirements of a righteous Jew. The Scripture does not tell us how Daniel reasoned about his situation, but it invites us to put ourselves in his place and work out our understanding of God’s sovereignty in our lives. 

Daniel likely saw the different levels of God's sovereign control, which provided at least three things: constraints, resources, and opportunities. The constraints of the various levels of God's sovereignty were obvious to Daniel. He did not choose his family, nation, or time in history. He did not select the economic conditions of his life or voluntarily choose to serve the pagan country of Babylon. Beyond constraints, God provided manifold, though finite, resources for Daniel's life. Such resources would include baseline intellectual capacities and his genetic inheritance. Daniel’s God-given resources would also include his family’s wealth or poverty, though this ceased to help him once he became a captive. Daniel’s resources were stewarded and increased through his diligence in understanding Scripture and God’s design for him as a believer. 

At times, Daniel may have thought God seemed remote from him, which is something most believers experience. But through his persistent study of God’s word and prayer, he learned to trust God’s sovereign direction not only for his own life but also for the lives and destiny of his fellow Jews and their nation. Daniel concerned himself with how he, his friends, fellow Jews, and the various kings he served responded to God. 

Opportunities differ from constraints. From any person’s perspective, opportunities may appear random. Such opportunities are what theologians would call Providence, where God provides for His loyal followers, sometimes even those who don’t follow Him. For Daniel, his opportunities arose from the very constraints of his captivity. Daniel had prepared himself spiritually for God's providential actions in Babylon through his earlier spiritual preparation. There is some evidence that we can neurologically escape our genetic constraints within limits, with new genetic expressions. 

Daniel and his friends were aware of the need to obey authority, knowing that disobeying authority equates to a form of disobeying God. They would have been aware of the social norms of their day, and Daniel exemplified hard work in his years of service as if he was serving God and not man. While we work for those who employ us, we fundamentally work for God. As Jesus says, we are to let our light shine before others, that they may see our good deeds and glorify our Father in heaven. 

Daniel seemed to know that even though he was under years of faithfulness to God, diligence, and excellence in his work, he was considered highly esteemed in the kingdom of God. His faithfulness in all these things led his acts to be included in the roll call of the faithful deeds of those who lived for God. God’s sovereignty is not constrained by events beyond His control in a fallen world. He clearly understood that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. While he was sifted with trials, he worked hard on his studies and improved his skills. Daniel was very adept at learning, and he excelled in political administration. He was found many times to be superior to the others. He succeeded by subordinating the priorities of his daily life to God's kingdom purposes.
