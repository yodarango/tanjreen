Daniel’s First Appeal: More Vegetables

The first chapter is set during the initial phase of captivity for Daniel and his three friends. The text begins by giving the reader a timestamp of when the events occur—in the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim, king of Judah. This ties the events to the Babylonian conquest of Egypt at Carchemish that year. Though Babylon primarily wanted to eliminate Egypt’s support for the Assyrian Empire and Assyria's control over the area of Palestine and Syria, the spoils of war allowed Babylon to take booty and people from the minor powers in the region, such as Syrians, Phoenicians, Egyptians, and Judeans.

The story unfolds dramatically, as they marched some nine hundred miles. Stripped of his ethnic identity, Daniel and his friends were likely emasculated. They were given new names, enrolled in the Babylonian administration, and trained for three years at the royal court.

Then the king ordered Ashpenaz, the chief of the eunuchs, to bring in some of the Israelites, specifically those without any physical defect, who were well-informed, showing aptitude for every kind of learning, and qualified to serve in the king’s palace. They were to be taught the language and literature of the Babylonians and assigned a daily amount of food and wine from the king’s table.

Daniel, whose name means "God is my judge," was given the name Belteshazzar, meaning "Bel (Marduk) protect his life." Mishael, meaning "Who is what God is," was renamed Meshach, meaning "Who is what Aku is." Hananiah, meaning "The Lord shows grace," was given the name Shadrach, meaning "command of Aku," and Azariah, meaning "The Lord helps," was renamed Abednego, meaning "servant of Nego/Nebo."

Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the royal food and wine, and he asked the chief official for permission not to do so. God had caused the official to show favor and sympathy to Daniel, but the official expressed concern that Daniel would look worse than the other young men. Daniel then said to the guard whom the chief official had appointed over him, "Test your servants for ten days: Give us nothing but vegetables to eat and water to drink. Then compare our appearance with that of the young men who eat the royal food, and treat your servants in accordance with what you see."

Daniel’s treatment at the hands of the Babylonians would have been against his will. Still, he selected a way to establish a boundary or separation from the Babylonian religious and social customs by requesting a different diet. Most commentators say that Daniel decided to take a stand over the king's choice of food because it was used in pagan sacrificial rites. Many commentators seem to assume that the Babylonians used food in pagan sacrifices. Daniel's objection to the food because of this use is a reasonable inference from New Testament passages, where Paul discusses how pagan converts to Christianity were concerned about eating meat sacrificed to idols.

However, it is worth noting that Daniel never explicitly states that the food they were to be given was sacrificed to pagan gods. Some historical records indicate that the king’s choice food was first sacrificed to the gods, and leftovers were then distributed to the king and his court. More importantly, Daniel eats meat and drinks wine later in life, suggesting that he may have appealed for a temporary, albeit necessary, expedient.

Daniel may have had two reasons for his appeal. The first would be to respect the dietary laws of Israel by avoiding proscribed foods if Babylon served them. The second would be out of an abundance of caution regarding Babylonian social pressure. He likely avoided eating proscribed meat for the first reason and drinking wine for the second.

First, though he would be living in a foreign land, Daniel would have wanted to adhere to Levitical laws, which specified the types of food acceptable to a religious Jew. These laws also forbade a religious Israelite from consuming meat with its blood or cooked with milk. There are no records of what the Jewish religious community in exile did to ensure adherence to the law.
